\begin{abstract}
Sub-terahertz (sub-THz) communication is a candidate technology for future sixth-generation (6G)-and-beyond networks, promising to deliver Terabit-per-second data rates with microsecond-level latencies. However, the reliance on highly directional "pencil beams" to overcome severe path loss introduces challenges in maintaining link alignment, particularly in mobile scenarios. While proactive, sensor-assisted beam management strategies exist, they are often too slow to prevent drops from unpredictable motion. This challenge is compounded by a lack of evaluation platforms capable of modeling high-mobility scenarios with real-time sensor integration.To address these challenges, we introducing the PanicPacket, a reactive protocol designed to preemptively realign the link \textit{just-in-time} before failure, and the MobileTHz platform, a C++ based simulation and Hardware-in-the-Loop environment for mobile THz systems. PanicPacket's efficacy is demonstrated through an experimental evaluation against a conventional blind search and a sensor-assisted reactive algorithm. By preemptively preventing link failures, PanicPacket significantly improves Mean Throughput and Throughput Stability, maximizes Link Availability, and maintains low Signaling Overhead. Together, the PanicPacket protocol and MobileTHz platform provide the research community with a robust solution and a versatile tool to advance the development of resilient, mobile sub-THz systems for 6G and beyond.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

The trajectory of wireless communications is defined by a demand for higher data throughput, lower latency, and connectivity\cite{Dang2020, DeAlwis2021, 9766110}. However, as the use of high-definition video streaming, cloud-based services, and the Internet of Things (IoT) continues to accelerate, the spectrum resources  allocated for 5G, primarily in the sub-6 GHz and millimeter-wave (mmWave) bands, are projected to become a significant bottleneck\cite{fi14040117}. Future applications such as immersive extended reality (XR), large-scale digital twinning, and integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) systems require peak data rates reaching the Terabit-per-second (Tbps) scale, coupled with latencies on the order of microseconds\cite{10422992, Azari2022, Nokia2022, DeAlwis2021, Samsung2020}. To address this need for network capacity, the research community has looked towards the sub-Terahertz (sub-THz) bands, spanning 0.1 THz to 1 THz as the possible next frontiers for wireless communication systems\cite{Kleine-Ostmann2011, Dang2020, DeAlwis2021, Samsung2020, Rappaport2019, 10494372}. One of the main attractions of the sub-THz spectrum is the availability of large, contiguous blocks of unallocated bandwidth, offering up to hundreds of gigahertz of usable spectrum\cite{10171235, 9766110}. This spectrum provides one of the keys to achieve Tbps-level data rates\cite{Nokia2022}.

The viability of THz communications is rapidly moving from theoretical speculation to practical demonstration\cite{Jornet2023}. Recent experimental milestones have showcased offline-system with impressive throughputs, including 1.0488 Tbps transmissions in the 330-500 GHz band, successful long-distance links, such as an 18 Gbps link over 30.2 km\cite{Yu2025Research}, and real-time software-defined radios (SDR) that shown 33 Gbps data-rates using the 135GHz sub-THz band\cite{9536953,10704668}. Furthermore, the formalization of IEEE 802.15.3d showcases the growing maturity in this technology, solidifying its position as a cornerstone of future 6G-and-beyond systems\cite{9269931,10494372}.

While the THz spectrum offers a solution to bandwidth scarcity, the properties of the THz channel present many challenges. At these frequencies, free-space path loss acts as significant impairment\cite{10422992, Yu2025Research, Kleine-Ostmann2011}. Additionally, THz signals exhibit poor diffraction and penetration capabilities, making them sensitive to blockage by common obstacles, such as a human hand or body, which are enough to sever a communication link\cite{10494372, Jornet2023}. To counteract these losses, THz systems must employ highly directional, high-gain "pencil beams" that concentrate radiated power towards the intended receiver\cite{Kleine-Ostmann2011}. However, this solution introduces a vulnerability: the directionality that makes THz links possible also makes them intolerant to misalignment. A deviation of just a few degrees between the transmitter's and receiver's beams can lead to complete loss of the received signal, destroying the connection. Sensitivity to alignment is one of the key challenges for making mobile THz communication systems a reality\cite{10036372, Petrov2023Mobile, Azari2022}.

The process of establishing and maintaining this alignment, known as beam management, is a performance bottleneck for mobile THz networks. It encompasses several procedures, including initial beam establishment, beam tracking to compensate for mobility, and beam failure recovery to re-establish a link after an interruption\cite{Giordani2018ATO, 10422712, Wang2022}. These tasks are difficult in mobile contexts where narrow beams are used and the channel changes frequently with user movement\cite{9536953, smartcities8040105}. In response, research has progressed from reactive, RF-only methods toward proactive strategies that use auxiliary information. Early reactive models used the history of the channel, often with statistical filters like the Kalman filter\cite{8455289,9145366,10067030, 10123123}. These models perform well for smooth, linear motion but are less effective with abrupt movements like rotation\cite{Wang2022}. This has motivated a shift toward proactive beam management, enabled by the 6G concept of ISAC\cite{10494372, Azari2022, Nokia2022, Chen2023, Jornet2023}. One approach is to augment network infrastructure with dedicated sensors like Radar, LiDAR, or cameras to provide real-time data on the location and trajectory of users and obstacles\cite{10422712,5GPPP_Automotive_Vision_2015}. Another approach leverages the user equipment (UE) itself, using on-device sensors like the Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) to provide low-latency measurements of device motion. Notably, sensor fusion techniques, which combine IMU data with RF measurements have been shown to improve tracking accuracy compared to using either data source alone \cite{10036372,9391649,electronics8020142, s21082723, 9814507}.

Proactive systems, whether infrastructure-centric or user-centric, have inherent limitations. The process of acquiring, processing, and transmitting sensor data introduces latency, making unpredictable mobility an issue\cite{9838772}. Furthermore, systems often rely on models trained on historical data, which may not account for sudden, unexpected events that deviate from learned patterns. This creates a \emph{prediction gap}, where an mobility event occurs too quickly for the proactive system to respond\cite{10036372}. Failure to respond results in a link drop, which requires a slow and resource-intensive recovery procedure. The high penalty for a missed prediction justifies the development of a ultra-low latency system designed to handle imminent link failures.

The development of such systems is further impeded by a gap in evaluation methodologies. While several simulators for THz communications exist, they are not designed to model the real-time interactions between sensor data, on-device algorithms, and physical-layer beam control in mobile scenarios. Early platforms like Terasim pioneered the modeling of frequency-dependent path loss but do not consider specific modulation and coding schemes, preventing a full analysis of link-level performance under mobility~\cite{HOSSAIN2019100004, eckhardt2022modular}. More recent channel modelers like NYUSIM are based on extensive real-world measurements but are limited to frequencies below 150~GHz and bandwidths of 1~GHz, which is insufficient for many 6G use cases~\cite{10367974}. SiMoNe, a simulator for mobile networks, attempts to cover this gap with a THz module. However, the module implements the IEEE 802.15.3d standard, which lacks protocols for beam tracking and alignment, thereby restricting the simulator to fixed point-to-point links only~\cite{rose2015simone, eckhardt2022modular}. Similarly, the recent TeraMIMO simulator, while accounting for near-field effects, is also limited to point-to-point links and cannot model interference or the antenna array architecture~\cite{tarboush2021teramimo, bodet2023directional}. Collectively, these tools are restricted to static or quasi-static links, which prevents the analysis of beam tracking with user movement~\cite{Petrov2023Mobile}. Additionally, while various hardware platforms have been developed for antenna alignment, they primarily target other frequencies, including low-cost Arduino-based systems in the microwave band~\cite{Abdulwahab2020}, robotic trackers at 28GHz mmWave\cite{Keshavamurthy2022}, and auto-tracking gimbals for free-space optical links~\cite{AlAkkoumi2008}. \emph{Hence, to the best of our knowledge, there is a distinct lack of both low-latency, link-saving algorithms and the integrated simulation-experimental platforms required to develop and validate them in realistic mobile THz scenarios.}

Motivated by the gaps in proactive algorithms and evaluation methodologies, this work introduces the PanicPacket algorithm and the MobileTHz platform. To demonstrate the efficacy of our approach, we present the design, implementation, and evaluation of three distinct beam alignment strategies. These include a baseline alignment algorithm, representing a conventional reactive method; an IMU-assisted algorithm, which improves upon the baseline by incorporating sensor data in a reactive manner and the PanicPacket algorithm, which uses a fusion of RF and IMU data to predict misalignment 'just-in-time' and coordinate a preemptive realignment before the link is lost. This analysis is enabled by MobileTHz, a high-performance C++ simulation and experimental platform we developed to address the limitations of existing tools. MobileTHz is designed to evaluate beam-management algorithms with a microsecond-level temporal resolution, with support for multiple antenna architectures, and support for integrated sensor data. Furthermore, the platform provides an experimental framework through a Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) capability, enabling the validation of simulation models against real-world data and testing algorithm performance using live inputs from physical sensors and THz transceivers.

\section*{Vision of the PanicPacket Protocol}

% To address the challenge of maintaining beam alignment in mobile THz networks, we envision a system composed of a stationary Access Point (AP) and a mobile User Equipment (UE) equipped with an on-board Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/vision_PanicPacket/panicpacketvision.drawio.pdf}
\vspace{-25 pt}
\caption{PanicPacket Protocol Vision}
\label{fig_alignment_pdf}
\vspace{-15 pt}
\end{figure}

The core vision is a just-in-time failure prevention mechanism built around a specialized message, the 'PanicPacket'. The sub-THz spectrum enables this vision; with large contiguous bandwidths that allow for extremely short symbol durations, making it possible to transmit packets on a microsecond timescale and thus react to mobility events before the channel changes. PanicPacket is low-latency protocol designed with three key principles: 1) minimize on-device processing at the user equipment (UE) to expedite packet creation, 2) offload time-consuming computations to the access point (AP), and 3) forgo computationally expensive methods like Kalman filters to ensure the entire reaction operates at microsecond-level latency.

The PanicPacket protocol compensates for the Access Point's (AP) lack of knowledge about User Equipment (UE) movement, which otherwise limits system performance. It prevents imminent link failures during critical moments using two separate algorithms for the UE and AP.

The UE's IMU is leveraged to detect motion significant enough to threaten the link before the link is lost. A stationary, Zero Velocity Update (ZUPT) phase is used to calibrate sensor biases of the accelerometer ($\sigma_a$) and gyroscope ($\sigma_{\omega}$), distinguishing link-critical movements from transient noise and mitigating drift. A `PanicPacket` is triggered when a unified ``Link Deviation Velocity'', $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$, projects an angular displacement that exceeds a fraction ($\alpha$) of the antenna's beamwidth ($\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$) over the system's reaction time ($T_{\text{react}}$). This metric combines the UE's rotational and translational velocity perpendicular to the line-of-sight, scaled by the link distance ($d$). This creates a trigger condition sensitive to both movement type and link geometry:
\begin{equation}
    \|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\| \cdot T_{\text{react}} > \alpha \cdot \theta_{\text{HPBW}}
\end{equation}
Upon a trigger, the UE transmits its integrated displacement and a buffer of recent IMU samples in a `PanicPacket` structured according to the IEEE 802.15.3d standard.

The AP receives this packet and performs its own forward prediction to compensate for reaction time. It processes the buffered IMU samples by applying an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA), providing a noise-filtered acceleration vector ($\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{predict}}$), This trend is used to calculate the displacement during the realignment interval, which is added to the integrated displacement from the UE. The AP converts this total predicted displacement into the required steering angles and immediately realigns its antenna, pointing to the UE \textit{was}, but where it is predicted to \textit{be}, thereby averting the link failure.

To validate the efficacy of this protocol, its performance is quantified against both a baseline reactive search and an IMU-assisted reactive algorithm. The evaluation focuses on key metrics including Mean Data Rate, Throughput Stability, Link Availability, Time to Realign, and the associated Signaling Overhead. The following analysis will demonstrate the protocol's benefits across a range of conditions, including high-acceleration motion, varied link geometries, and different antenna configurations.

\section*{Results}

In this study, four major effects on beam alignment in mobile sub-THz scenarios are explored. The first is the effect of translational acceleration on protocol performance. The second is the effect of high rotational acceleration, which presents a significant challenge to conventional alignment strategies. Third, we illustrate how the initial link geometry, specifically separation distance and total angular displacement, impacts the performance of each protocol. Finally, the impact of antenna directivity on alignment robustness under a combined translational and rotational mobility scenario is explored, modelling a more realistic mobility pattern.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------%
\subsection*{Effects of Translational Acceleration on Beam Alignment}

To investigate the impact of translational acceleration, we analyze a scenario where the User Equipment (UE) is displaced by 1.5 meters from an initial separation of 3 meters from the Access Point (AP). Each trial begins with the AP and UE perfectly aligned, a condition that establishes a performance floor; the average throughput is dictated by the total displacement and its speed, preventing a complete drop to zero. This lower bound is benchmarked by the 'Static' scenario, where no alignment adjustments are made. The peak translational acceleration, ranging from 0.5 to 20\,m/s$^2$, is the independent variable. For a fixed displacement, lower peak accelerations result in longer scenario durations. This range is selected to reflect realistic mobility patterns, from pedestrian flows (0.68--1.44\,m/s$^2$) to maximum vehicular accelerations (~2.87\,m/s$^2$) and high-intensity human motion (~9.5\,m/s$^2$). The evaluation is conducted under two distinct steering models: an \textit{ideal steering scenario} simulating high-performance kinematics, and a second scenario modeling the physical limitations of the \textit{experimental equipment}.

The average throughput is calculated as the time-average of the instantaneous channel capacity, $C(t)$, derived from the Shannon-Hartley theorem over the scenario's duration:
\begin{equation}
    C(t) = B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}(t))
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the channel bandwidth and $\text{SNR}(t)$ is the instantaneous signal-to-noise ratio.

Figure \ref{fig:trans_sim_rate} showcases the performance of the ideal steering scenario, the PanicPacket protocol exhibits a near-linear decrease in average throughput as peak acceleration increases but consistently outperforms the other algorithms. The IMU-assisted algorithm initially shows lower performance than the Baseline, a result of its aggressive prediction model which is optimized for high-mobility dynamics and less suited to low-velocity regimes. As acceleration increases, this predictive approach becomes advantageous; the throughput plateaus around 50\,Gbps, benefiting from a combination of shorter scenario times, which reduce the number of required prediction updates, and the increased efficacy of its time-dependent prediction. The Baseline algorithm, relying solely on channel state information, maintains stable performance up to 2\,m/s$^2$ before its throughput degrades in a logarithmic fashion as reactive search latency becomes a bottleneck.

When modeling the physical limitations of the experimental equipment (Figure \ref{fig:trans_exp_rate}), the limited rotational velocity and acceleration impose a high opportunity cost on every movement. An incorrect initial move leads to prolonged misalignment. Consequently, the IMU-assisted and PanicPacket protocols excel by incorporating motion data. The PanicPacket initially achieves the highest throughput, which then decreases logarithmically to 52\,Gbps as the user's velocity outpaces the system's ability to execute realignment before the SNR drops below the threshold required for successful packet decoding. The IMU-assisted algorithm demonstrates clear gains over the Baseline after 2\,m/s$^2$ and plateaus around 45\,Gbps. The Baseline algorithm, lacking the agility to track the UE, performs equivalently to a static configuration.

While average throughput is a primary metric, the benefits of motion-aware beam management are apparent when looking at link quality, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:translational_metrics}. Link stability is quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV) of the throughput:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CV} = \frac{\sigma_R}{\mu_R}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_R$ and $\sigma_R$ are the mean and standard deviation of the throughput, respectively. CV is advantageous as it normalizes fluctuation against the average signal strength, providing a clearer picture of stability than standard deviation alone. As seen in Figure \ref{fig:trans_metrics_covariance}, the Baseline algorithm exhibits high instability, with a CV of 0.8 that increases above 3\,m/s$^2$. The IMU-assisted algorithm improves stability, hovering around a CV of 0.3, while the PanicPacket protocol maintains a highly stable link with a CV around 0.1.

Link outage, defined as the cumulative time the link SNR is below 20\,dB, is presented in Figure \ref{fig:trans_metrics_outage}. Both the Baseline and IMU-assisted algorithms frequently fail to align their main lobes, providing insufficient antenna gain to close the link budget. In contrast, the PanicPacket protocol sustains the link with minimal outages until 5\,m/s$^2$, after which the outage duration increases linearly to 4 seconds at 20\,/s$^2$.

Finally, the signaling overhead of the PanicPacket protocol is evaluated as an opportunity cost—the potential throughput lost during the time dedicated to packet transmission, which could have been used for higher-order data modulation at high SNR. Figure \ref{fig:trans_metrics_overhead} shows that this overhead decreases logarithmically with acceleration. This is because higher accelerations lead to shorter scenario durations, which in turn reduces the number of motion events significant enough to trigger a packet transmission.

\subsection*{Effects of Rotational Acceleration on Beam Alignment}

Rotational motion presents a greater challenge to beam management than pure translation, as typical angular accelerations can be significantly higher, leading to shorter channel coherence times. Furthermore, the optimal alignment strategy for a rotational-only scenario is for the mobile UE to counter-rotate while the stationary AP remains fixed on its initial bearing. This is counter-intuitive for algorithms designed for translational tracking, as they cannot easily distinguish between motion types from RF data alone and may initiate a detrimental search pattern. The acceleration values used in this analysis are based on empirical IMU measurements of human motion, as detailed in the Appendix.

As shown in the idealized steering scenario (Figure \ref{fig:rot_sim_rate}), algorithms optimized for a cooperative, two-sided search are counterproductive. The Baseline algorithm, by design, moves the AP away from its optimal, static pointing direction, causing its performance to be on par with, or even worse than, a static link. The IMU-assisted algorithm improves upon this as the UE's onboard sensor allows it to react and counter-rotate before the AP can significantly misalign itself. Consequently, as rotational speed increases and scenario duration decreases, the IMU-assisted algorithm's performance improves relative to the Baseline because the AP has less opportunity to execute its incorrect search. The PanicPacket protocol provides the best performance, as it correctly identifies the rotational movement and prevents the AP from initiating a search, thereby maintaining a high-throughput link.

The scenario constrained by the experimental equipment (Figure \ref{fig:rot_exp_rate}) highlights the challenge posed by high angular acceleration. The required speeds quickly exceed the mechanical limits of the rotary tables, causing all algorithms to converge toward a non-connected noise floor above 400\,deg/s$^2$. Here, both the Baseline and IMU-assisted methods fail to maintain a connection. The PanicPacket protocol, however, exhibits a more graceful logarithmic decrease, even managing to sustain some throughput in the 100-150\,deg/s$^2$ range, which is beyond the equipment's maximum theoretical acceleration of 60\,deg/s$^2$.

Link availability in the idealized scenario, shown in Figure \ref{fig:rot_metrics_availability}, reveals further nuances. While the IMU-assisted algorithm achieves a respectable average throughput, its link availability is poor because it cannot ensure alignment between the main lobes of both antennas. The UE successfully points its main lobe, but the misaligned AP receives the signal via a lower-gain side lobe, resulting in an SNR below the 20\,dB threshold for a successful link. The PanicPacket protocol, by keeping both nodes correctly oriented, maintains a usable link with over 80\% availability up to approximately 75\,rad/s$^2$. The cost of maintaining this alignment is shown in Figure \ref{fig:rot_metrics_overhead}. Due to the short channel coherence time, the opportunity cost of sending packets is lower than in translational scenarios. The signaling overhead peaks at around 80\,Mbits in low-acceleration scenarios and decreases as the scenario duration shortens and fewer packets are successfully decoded due to rapid SNR degradation.

\subsection*{Effects of Link Geometry on Mobile Performance}

To complement the analysis of acceleration, we now investigate how the initial link geometry affects performance under fixed motion profiles. Two scenarios are considered: the first varies the initial separation distance during a translational maneuver, and the second varies the total angular displacement during a rotational maneuver.

In the translational scenario, the UE is displaced by 1.5\,m at a fixed peak acceleration of 3.0\,m/s$^2$, while the initial separation distance is varied from 1 to 20 meters. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:distance_effects}a, the theoretical maximum throughput follows the expected power decay with distance, dropping sharply after 15 meters as the link budget becomes insufficient to maintain an SNR above 3\,dB. The Baseline algorithm exhibits its worst performance at close proximity. At these short distances, the fixed displacement of 1.5 \,m subtends a large angle, significantly reducing channel coherence time and leading to frequent missed alignments. The IMU-assisted algorithm improves upon this, primarily through more consistent and successful realignments. The PanicPacket protocol offers further gains but is still constrained by the short coherence time at sub-4-meter distances. A notable convergence occurs around 4.5 meters, where the increasing distance lengthens the channel coherence time. This allows the Baseline's reactive search to become more effective, closing the gap with the IMU-assisted algorithm, though neither achieves the theoretical maximum due to residual link instability.

Figure \ref{fig:distance_effects}b quantifies this link stability using the throughput's coefficient of variation (CV). The IMU-enabled algorithms provide an approximate two-fold improvement in stability over the Baseline, which suffers from extremely chaotic link behavior at close ranges. As the separation distance increases towards 4.5 meters, all algorithms converge to their most stable state, with the PanicPacket protocol achieving a near-zero CV, while the others reach a global minimum around 0.25.

For the ultra-narrow 1.3° beam, the link becomes untenable even at low mobility, with availability dropping before the 1\,m/s$^2$ and 40\,deg/s$^2$ threshold. This underscores a critical design principle: maximizing antenna gain is not the optimal strategy for mobility. The beam management protocol and antenna hardware must be co-designed to strike a balance between sufficient gain and a manageable beamwidth that accommodates system latencies.

\section*{Discussion}

The results presented in the previous section provide critical insights into the challenges of beam management in mobile sub-THz systems and validate the performance of the proposed PanicPacket protocol. The key findings and their implications are discussed across three main themes.

\emph{First}, the operational model of reactive beam management is fundamentally limited by latency. Both the Baseline and IMU-assisted algorithms, which trigger alignment after detecting link degradation, consistently underperform. Their recovery time, composed of failure detection and subsequent searching, frequently exceeds the channel coherence time, particularly during rotational motion. This is quantitatively observed in the mechanically constrained scenarios (e.g., Figure \ref{fig:trans_exp_rate}), where the high cost of incorrect steering movements causes these reactive protocols to yield a lower average throughput than a static, non-steered link. The core issue is that their post-failure recovery architecture is ill-suited for the rapid channel dynamics of mobile sub-THz links.

\emph{Second}, a just-in-time, proactive protocol effectively mitigates the latency limitations of reactive systems. The PanicPacket protocol's performance gains are directly attributable to its preemptive mechanism. By transmitting UE motion data just before a predicted misalignment, it allows the AP to make a corrective alignment before the link SNR drops below a functional threshold. This process bypasses the entire failure-detection-and-recovery loop. The resulting performance improvement is substantial, with a measured 6-fold increase in average throughput in rotational scenarios (Figure \ref{fig:rotational_rate}) and a 7-fold improvement in throughput stability (Figure \ref{fig:trans_metrics_covariance}). This confirms that minimizing system reaction time by proactively communicating state information is a viable and highly effective strategy.

\emph{Third}, the optimal antenna directivity is not an independent parameter but is coupled with the beam management protocol's performance characteristics. The results in Figure \ref{fig:antenna_comparison} show that maximizing antenna gain is a suboptimal strategy for mobility. For the PanicPacket protocol, the 9° HPBW antenna provided the best performance, balancing the link budget benefits of higher gain against the alignment tolerance of a wider beam. In contrast, for the less accurate reactive algorithms, the widest 13° beam was always superior. An absolute limit was observed with the 1.3° HPBW antenna, where the angular rate of change exceeded the system's end-to-end reaction latency, causing immediate link failure even with the PanicPacket protocol. This highlights a critical co-design requirement: the antenna system must be selected based on the alignment accuracy and reaction time of the specific beam management protocol in use.

\emph{In conclusion}, this work quantifies the performance degradation of reactive beam management in mobile sub-THz channels and validates a novel just-in-time proactive protocol that overcomes these limitations. The consistent and significant performance gains of the PanicPacket protocol establish it as a robust solution for enabling mobile 6G systems. The MobileTHz platform, used for this analysis, provides the necessary high-fidelity environment to model these complex system interactions and serves as a valuable tool for future research in this domain.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Methods/algorithm_baseline/baseline.pdf}
    \vspace{-20 pt}
    \caption{Baseline blind search trajectory in blue, with antenna HPBW radiation trace shown in between anchor points.}

\end{wrapfigure}
The evaluation of the proposed beam management algorithms is conducted using the MobileTHz platform, an environment designed to model and test sub-THz communication systems in high-mobility scenarios. The platform comprises a modular C++ simulation engine and a Hardware-in-the-Loop experimental framework, allowing for testing of beam management algorithms.
Additionaly,  this platform allows for testing of various mobility scenarios in simulation and experimental environments. To evaluate the benefits of the PanicPacket, we designed and implemented two additional beam alignment algorithms, representing a progression from a conventional reactive approach to our proposed proactive system. These algorithms are the baseline reactive search and the IMU-assisted reactive search.

\subsection*{Baseline Reactive Search}
The Baseline Reactive Search initiates when a link failure is detected, which occurs when a short-term Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of received power drops below a threshold, deteremined by the antennas half-power beamwidth (HPBW).
Upon detection, the AP initiates a blind recovery search using an optimized discrete spiral, adapted from optical systems~\cite{photonics11060540}. Our method exponentially expands the search area to account for the growing uncertainty in the UE's position over time. The step size for each successive ring of the spiral, $S_k$, grows from an initial step size, $S_0$, which proportional to the antenna's HPBW:
\begin{equation}
    S_k = S_0 \cdot \gamma^k,
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the ring index and $\gamma > 1$ is a growth factor.
he search continues until the signal recovers above a dynamic success threshold that becomes more lenient over time to accommodate larger displacements. The required power for success, $P_{succ}(t)$, is defined relative to the last known-good reference power, $P_{ref}$, by a time-dependent margin, $M(\Delta t)$:
\begin{equation}
    P_{succ}(\Delta t) = P_{ref} - M(\Delta t) = P_{ref} - (M_{initial} + R \cdot \Delta t),
\end{equation}
where $M_{initial}$ is the initial margin and $R$ is a margin increase rate. Once the link is re-established, a final "peak seeking" phase fine-tunes the alignment by settling on the local power maximum before the search concludes.

\subsection*{IMU-assisted Reactive Search}

This algorithm replaces the blind search with an informed, sensor-driven approach. The same power-drop trigger is used to initiate recovery but leverages the UE's on-board IMU to localize the search. The IMU is used to estimate the UE's movement since the link was stable. The UE processes accelerometer data by integration to produce a translational displacement vector, $\Delta\mathbf{p}(t)$ and gyroscope data to track changes in orientation. This information is used to predict the new line-of-sight (LoS) by computing the target azimuth and altitude angles: 
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\text{az, target}}(t) = \theta_{\text{az,0}} + \arctan\left(\frac{\Delta p_{y, \text{local}}}{d_0}\right) - \Delta\psi(t)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\text{alt, target}}(t) = \theta_{\text{alt,0}} + \arctan\left(\frac{\Delta p_{z, \text{local}}}{d_0}\right) + \Delta\phi(t)
\end{equation}
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\linewidth}
    \centering
    \vspace{50 pt}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Methods/algorithm_IMUreact/imu.pdf}
    \caption{The blind search trajectory for the baseline algorithm. The path shows the area swept by the antenna beam (HPBW), with a time-based gradient from black (start) to gray (end). The axes are normalized by the beamwidth ($\theta_{\mathrm{HPBW}}$).}
    \label{fig:baseline_search}
    \vspace{-15 pt}
\end{wrapfigure}
where $\theta_{\text{az,0}}$ and $\theta_{\text{alt,0}}$ are the last known-good angles; $\Delta p_{y, \text{local}}$ and $\Delta p_{z, \text{local}}$ are the horizontal and vertical components of displacement perpendicular to the LoS; $d_0$ is the link distance; and $\Delta\psi(t)$ and $\Delta\phi(t)$ are the changes in yaw and pitch, respectively. The UE then steers directly to this predicted angle. Because IMU integration is prone to drift and compounded errors, this initial prediction is not perfect and prone to disruptions from noise. Therefore, after steering to the predicted angle, the algorithm initiates a tight, spiral search with a small step size, $S_{\text{local}}$:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{local}} = \alpha \cdot \theta_{\text{HPBW}}, \quad (\text{where } 0 < \alpha \ll 1)
\end{equation}
This localized search, where every step is a small fraction ($\alpha$) of the beamwidth ($\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$), corrects for sensor errors and allows the UE to settle on the precise power peak.

\subsection*{Generation of Mobility Scenarios}
To evaluate algorithm performance under controlled conditions, we defined three primary mobility scenarios. In each scenario, a stationary Access Point (AP) is located at the origin, and a mobile User Equipment (UE) begins at a fixed initial separation distance, with both devices pointing directly at each other.

In a translational scenario, the UE is displaced by a specified distance, $D$, over a set duration, $T$. The intensity of the scenario can be tuned by varying the initial separation distance, the total displacement $D$, and the time $T$. As detailed in the following section, these parameters directly determine the maximum acceleration required for the movement.

In an rotational scenario, the UE rotates by a specified number of degrees over a set duration, $T$. The challenge is similarly controlled by modifying the initial separation distance, the total angular displacement, and the time $T$, which dictates the maximum angular acceleration.

Finally, a hybrid scenario combines both translational and rotational movement simultaneously to simulate more complex, realistic user activity. The specific implementation of these scenarios within the simulation and experimental frameworks is detailed in the respective subsections below.

\subsection*{The MobileTHz Simulation Platform}
The MobileTHz platform discretizes time into slots, indexed by $n$, where the simulation time is $t = n \Delta t$ for a configurable slot duration $\Delta t$. This approach allows deterministic simulation of node kinematics, RF channel interactions, hardware behaviors, and algorithms presented in this work.

\begin{wrapfigure}{l}{0.2\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Methods/simulation_platform/MobileTHz_Platform.pdf}
    \caption{MobileTHz Software architecture.}
    \label{fig:baseline_search}
\end{wrapfigure}

A mobile scenario is consists of a set of mobile nodes, $\mathcal{M} = \{m_1, m_2, \dots\}$, whose trajectories are specified by a sequence of keyframes, $\{(\mathbf{p}_k, \mathbf{q}_k)\}_{k=0}^{K-1}$. Each keyframe defines the position $\mathbf{p}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and orientation (as a unit quaternion) $\mathbf{q}_k \in \mathbb{S}^3$ at a specific time slot $n_k$. To create smooth and realistic paths between these keyframes, the platform uses Perlin's Smoother Step function, $f(\tau)$:
\begin{equation}
f(\tau) = 6\tau^5 - 15\tau^4 + 10\tau^3,
\end{equation}
where $\tau = (t - t_k) / (t_{k+1} - t_k) \in [0, 1]$ is the normalized time. This function's C2 continuity ensures that velocity, acceleration, and jerk are zero at the keyframe endpoints, simulating natural motion. This approach also allows for the design of trajectories that respect physical constraints, where the maximum acceleration, $a_{max}$, for a transition over distance $D$ in duration $T$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    a_{max} = K \cdot \frac{D}{T^2}, \quad \text{where} \quad K \approx 5.7735.
\end{equation}

Additionally, a node's orientation is interpolated using Spherical Linear Interpolation (SLERP), which ensures a constant angular velocity rotation along the shortest path on the unit sphere of orientations:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Slerp}(\mathbf{q}_k, \mathbf{q}_{k+1}, \tau) = \frac{\sin((1-\tau)\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\mathbf{q}_k + \frac{\sin(\tau\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\mathbf{q}_{k+1},
\end{equation}
where $\Omega = \arccos(\mathbf{q}_k \cdot \mathbf{q}_{k+1})$. Linear velocity $\mathbf{v}[n]$ and acceleration $\mathbf{a}[n]$ are computed using central finite difference approximations. The angular velocity, $\boldsymbol{\omega}[n]$, is derived by examining the incremental rotation between consecutive time slots, captured by the difference quaternion $\mathbf{q}[n+1] \otimes \mathbf{q}[n]^{-1}$, whose vector part is then scaled to yield the angular velocity. Finally, the angular acceleration $\boldsymbol{\alpha}[n]$ is calculated as the finite difference of this angular velocity sequence.  This model of motion allows us to precalculate the position, velocity, and acceleration of each node at any discrete time slot $n$, which are inputs for modeling the RF channel, hardware components, and algorithm decisions.

Once a scenario is defined, the simulation engine executes a loop for a series of discrete time slots. The simulation models this link by accounting for both signal propagation through space and the directivity of the antennas. Signal propagation is modeled according to the Friis transmission equation, where the free-space path loss (FSPL) in dB is given by:
\begin{equation}
    L_{p} = 20 \log_{10}(d) + 20 \log_{10}(f_c) - 147.55,
\end{equation}
where $d = ||\mathbf{p}_{rx} - \mathbf{p}_{tx}||$ is the line-of-sight (LoS) distance in meters. In addition to path loss, received power is dependent on antenna directivity. This is based on a node's effective orientation, $\mathbf{q}_{eff}$, which is the Hamilton product of its body orientation $\mathbf{q}_{body}$ and, if a rotary-stage is used, its mechanical stage orientation $\mathbf{q}_{rotary}$. This effective orientation is used to determine the antenna gain by transforming the LoS vector into the antenna's local frame, obtained from a gain vs. theta angle radiation pattern.

The MobileTHz platform uses a high-fidelity model based on the mathematical representation of a sub-THz WR-6.5 conical horn antenna E-plane for antenna modeling. The gain amplitude pattern, $\text{G}_n(\theta)$, is given by:
\begin{equation}
    G_n(\theta) = \frac{1 + \cos(\theta)}{2} \sqrt{\frac{(C(r_2) - C(r_1))^2 + (S(r_2) - S(r_1))^2}{4(C(r_0)^2 + S(r_0)^2)}},
\end{equation}
where $C(x)$ and $S(x)$ are the Fresnel cosine and sine integrals, respectively, and $r_1$, $r_2$, and $r_0$ are parameters dependent on the antenna dimensions and the angle $\theta$. Since the antenna gain for each channel link is defined, the received power in dBm is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
P_{rx} = P_{tx} + G_{tx}(\theta_{tx}) + G_{rx}(\theta_{rx}) - L_p.
\end{equation}
To account for small-scale fading effects, the final received power, $p_{rx}$, is modeled as a random variable following an exponential distribution whose mean is the ideal linear received power.

The simulation then applies a hardware abstraction layer, modeling the constraints of physical components. A steerable antenna's orientation is controlled by a dual-axis mechanical stage, which adjusts the $\mathbf{q}_{rotary}$ component of the effective orientation. For any instructed angular displacement, $\Delta\theta$, the model uses a trapezoidal velocity profile defined as a piecewise function:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:accel_profile}
	a(t) =
	\begin{cases}
		+\alpha_{\text{max}} & \text{Phase I: Acceleration}       \\
		0               & \text{Phase II: Constant velocity} \\
		-\alpha_{\text{max}} & \text{Phase III: Deceleration}     \\
	\end{cases},
\end{equation}
where the duration of each phase is determined by $\Delta\theta$ and the stage's maximum velocity ($\omega_{max}$) and acceleration ($\alpha_{max}$) constraints.

After a physical change in orientation, there is an inherent latency before the RF front-end and digital processing pipeline can report a new power measurement to the controlling algorithm. This delay, representing signal acquisition, filtering, and digital conversion is an important factor in real-world systems. Therefore the model captures this with a configurable processing delay, $\tau_{proc}$, defined as an integer number of time slots. The power value available to the algorithm at any time $t$ within a given processing interval is held constant at the value measured at the beginning of the \textit{previous} interval.

While the RF chain provides feedback on the state of the communication link, an algorithm can also increase data diversity through the node's own physical motion to make informed control decisions. This data is provided by a simulated Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), a device that integrates a three sensors to measure motion. The gyroscope measures the node's angular velocity ($\boldsymbol{\omega}$), providing the primary data for tracking short-term changes in orientation. The accelerometer measures specific force ($\mathbf{f}$), which is the vector sum of the node's kinematic acceleration and the gravitational field, allowing the system to track translational motion. The magnetometer provides an absolute heading reference by measuring the local magnetic field, similar to a compass, which is crucial for correcting long-term orientation drift and establishing reference orientation.

Realistic IMU data is simulated by modifying the simulated environment kinematics with noise models for the two primary sensors used in tracking: the accelerometer and the gyroscope. The simulated sensor output, $\tilde{\mathbf{a}}[n]$, is affected by these error sources:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{a}}[n] = \mathbf{f}_{ideal}[n] + \mathbf{b}_{a}[n] + \mathbf{n}_{a}[n].
\end{equation}
The bias $\mathbf{b}_{a}[n]$ evolves as a random walk: $\mathbf{b}_{a}[n] = \mathbf{b}_{a}[n-1] + \mathbf{w}_{b,a}[n]$, where the update term $\mathbf{w}_{b,a} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{a,b}^2 \Delta t \cdot \mathbf{I}_3)$ and the white noise is modeled as $\mathbf{n}_{a}[n] \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (\sigma_{a,n}^2 / \Delta t) \cdot \mathbf{I}_3)$. A similar noise model is applied to the gyroscope readings, where the ideal measurement is the ground-truth angular velocity, $\boldsymbol{\omega}[n]$, and the simulated output, $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}[n]$, incorporates its own distinct bias and white noise components.

Finally, the resulting state of the node is fed to the beam management algorithm to make a control decision. This entire process is repeated for all time slots until the end of the scenario, at which point performance metrics are calculated. The parameters used in the simulation are configured to mimic the device properties of the experimental platform to ensure a close correspondence between results.

\subsection*{The Experimental Platform}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Methods/experimental_testbed/experimentalTestbed.drawio.pdf}
\caption{Experimental testbed}
\label{fig1_algorithms}
\end{figure}

The MobileTHz experimental platform serves as the Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) counterpart to the simulation environment, enabling the validation of beam management algorithms by substituting simulated components with measurements from physical hardware. This framework allows for the replacement of the simulated RF channel and sensor inputs with real-world data streams. For instance, received power values can be acquired directly from the RF hardware, and orientation data can be obtained from the rotary stage encoders, providing a testbed that bridges the gap between pure simulation and full-scale deployment.

The platform's RF chain is designed for operation in the sub-THz spectrum, specifically within the WR-6.5 waveguide band (110-170 GHz). The transmitter signal path starts with a Keysight E8257D Performance Signal Generator (PSG) that produces the local oscillator (LO) signal. Concurrently, a Keysight M8196A Arbitrary Waveform Generator (AWG) generates a 1 GHz sinusoidal intermediate frequency (IF) signal. Both signals are then processed by a Virginia Diodes, Inc. (VDI) mixer/amplifier/multiplier (MIXAMC) chain, which upconverts the signal to the desired RF frequency for transmission. The receiver chain mirrors this architecture, employing a corresponding VDI MIXAMC with an integrated low-noise amplifier (LNA) to downconvert the received signal. The resulting IF signal power is measured and digitized by a Keysight DSOZ632A Digital Storage Oscilloscope (DSO).

Mechanical beam steering is implemented using IntelLiDrives dual-axis rotary tables positioned under the transmitter and receiver front-ends. These tables utilize a worm gear drive with stepper motors, providing precise angular positioning with a resolution of up to 0.0005 degrees. This setup allows for the physical emulation of the steering mechanics modeled in the simulation. The RF chains are connected to one of two antenna types, used without supplementary lenses. The first is a VDI WR-6.5 standard conical horn antenna, characterized by a 21 dBi gain and a 13-degree half-power beamwidth (HPBW). The second is an Anteral HGHLA-WR06 high-gain horn antenna, which provides a 40 dBi gain and a much narrower 3-degree HPBW.

The control and orchestration of the hardware components are managed through a host computer. An in-house C++ library, RotaryLib, provides an application programming interface (API) to command the rotary tables' StepNet amplifiers via a USB-to-RS232 serial connection. This library enables the execution of timed movement profiles that replicate the trapezoidal velocity model used in the simulation. In an HIL configuration, power measurements from the DSO and position feedback from the rotary table encoders are streamed to the host computer. This data is then fed into the MobileTHz C++ engine, allowing algorithms to operate on real-time physical measurements instead of simulated values.

\subsection*{IMU Results}
To establish realistic parameters for the mobility scenarios used in this paper, we conducted two experiments to capture typical human motion profiles. An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) was affixed to a smartphone case to record its motion during common user activities.

The first experiment, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:imu_hand_motion}, involved a compound motion: raising the phone from a resting position perpendicular to the user's body up to their face, a typical gesture when answering a call or viewing the screen. This action was repeated twice, resulting in the two distinct patterns visible in the plots. This gesture involves both a significant translational component to lift the device (Fig.~\ref{fig:imu_hand_linear}) and a rotational component to orient it for viewing (Fig.~\ref{fig:imu_hand_angular}).

The second experiment, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:imu_body_rotation}, was designed to isolate rotational movement by having the user stand and rotate their body without moving the phone relative to their torso. This action was also repeated twice to gather baseline values for pure human rotation.

The results highlight the distinct characteristics of each motion type. For the hand-raising gesture, while rotational velocities are clearly identifiable and reach upwards of \SI{200}{\degree\per\second}, the translational accelerations are dominant, exceeding \SI{10}{\meter\per\second\squared} (\SI{1}{g}) as the lightweight device is rapidly moved. Conversely, during body rotation, rotational velocities are of a similar magnitude, but translational accelerations remain comparatively stable and low, primarily resulting from centripetal forces. These empirical data provide a grounded basis for the acceleration values modeled in our simulations.
