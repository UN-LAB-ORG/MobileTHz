\begin{abstract}
Sub-terahertz (sub-THz) communication is a candidate technology for sixth-generation (6G)-and-beyond networks, promising Terabit-per-second data rates with microsecond-level latencies. However, the highly directional "pencil beams" required to overcome severe path loss make maintaining link alignment under mobility challenging. Existing reactive, sensor-assisted beam management strategies are often too slow to prevent outages caused by unpredictable motion. Progress is further limited by the absence of evaluation platforms that jointly model high-mobility dynamics and real-time sensor integration. To address these challenges, we introduce PanicPacket, a proactive algorithm designed to preemptively realign the link \textit{just-in-time} before failure, and the MobileTHz platform, an integrated simulation and Hardware-in-the-Loop platform for mobile THz systems. Benchmarked against a conventional Blind Search and a sensor-assisted reactive algorithm, PanicPacket significantly improves mean capacity and stability, maximizes link availability, and maintains overhead below 0.3\% of channel capacity. Together, PanicPacket and MobileTHz provide a robust solution and versatile tool to advance resilient, mobile sub-THz systems for 6G and beyond.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

The trajectory of wireless communications is defined by a demand for higher data capacity, lower latency, and ubiquitous connectivity\cite{Dang2020, DeAlwis2021, 9766110}. However, as the use of high-definition video streaming, cloud-based services, and the Internet of Things (IoT) continues to accelerate, the spectrum resources  allocated for 5G, primarily in the sub-6 GHz and millimeter-wave (mmWave) bands, are projected to become a significant bottleneck\cite{fi14040117}. Future applications such as immersive extended reality (XR), large-scale digital twinning, and integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) systems ultimately require peak data rates reaching the Terabit-per-second (Tbps) scale, coupled with latencies on the order of microseconds\cite{10422992, Azari2022, Nokia2022, DeAlwis2021, Samsung2020}. To address this need for network capacity, the research community has looked towards the sub-Terahertz (sub-THz) region, spanning 0.1 THz to 1 THz as the next possible frontiers for wireless communication systems\cite{Kleine-Ostmann2011, Dang2020, DeAlwis2021, Samsung2020, Rappaport2019, 10494372}. The sub-THz spectrum offers large, contiguous blocks of unallocated bandwidth, offering up to hundreds of gigahertz of usable spectrum\cite{10171235, 9766110}. This spectrum provides one of the keys to achieve Tbps-level data rates\cite{Nokia2022}.

The viability of THz communications is rapidly moving from theoretical speculation to practical demonstration. Recent experimental milestones have showcased offline systems with impressive throughput, including 1.0488 Tbps transmissions in the 330-500 GHz band, successful long-distance links, such as an 18~Gbps link over 30.2 km\cite{Yu2025Research}, and real-time software-defined radios (SDR) that have demonstrated 33~Gbps data-rates using the 135 GHz sub-THz band\cite{9536953,10704668}.

Although the THz spectrum offers a solution to bandwidth scarcity, the properties of the THz channel present many challenges. At these frequencies, free-space path loss acts as a significant impairment\cite{10422992, Yu2025Research, Kleine-Ostmann2011}. Additionally, THz signals exhibit poor diffraction and penetration capabilities, making them sensitive to blockage by common obstacles, such as a human hand or body, which are enough to sever a communication link\cite{10494372, Jornet2023}. To counteract these losses, THz systems must employ highly directional, high-gain "pencil beams" that concentrate radiated power towards the intended receiver\cite{Kleine-Ostmann2011}. However, this solution introduces a vulnerability: the directionality that makes THz links possible also makes them intolerant to misalignment. A deviation of just a few degrees between the transmitter's and receiver's beams can lead to complete loss of the received signal, destroying the connection. Sensitivity to alignment is one of the key challenges for making mobile THz communication systems a reality\cite{10036372, Petrov2023Mobile, Azari2022}.

Maintaining beam alignment under mobility, commonly called beam management, is a key bottleneck for mobile THz networks\cite{Giordani2018ATO, 10422712, Wang2022}. Conventional reactive approaches, such as Kalman-filter-based tracking on RF measurements\cite{8455289,9145366,10067030, 10123123}, work well for smooth, linear motion but struggle with abrupt changes like device rotation\cite{Wang2022}. This has motivated a shift toward proactive strategies that incorporate auxiliary sensor data\cite{10494372, Azari2022, Nokia2022, Chen2023, Jornet2023}. Infrastructure-side sensors (radar, LiDAR, cameras) can track user position\cite{10422712,5GPPP_Automotive_Vision_2015}, while on-device Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) provide low-latency motion estimates directly at the user equipment (UE). Fusing IMU and RF data has been shown to outperform either source alone\cite{10036372,9391649,electronics8020142, s21082723, 9814507}.

Progress is further limited by a gap in evaluation tools. Existing THz simulators, including TeraSim\cite{HOSSAIN2019100004}, NYUSIM\cite{10367974}, SiMoNe\cite{rose2015simone, eckhardt2022modular}, and TeraMIMO\cite{tarboush2021teramimo, bodet2023directional}, are restricted to static or quasi-static point-to-point links and lack support for real-time beam tracking under mobility\cite{Petrov2023Mobile}. Additionally, although hardware alignment platforms exist for lower frequency bands\cite{Abdulwahab2020, Keshavamurthy2022, AlAkkoumi2008}, they do not address the unique constraints of sub-THz systems. \emph{To the best of our knowledge, there is a distinct lack of both low-latency, link-saving algorithms and the integrated simulation-experimental platforms required to develop and validate them in realistic mobile THz scenarios.}

Although inertial sensors have been used for reactive realignment\cite{9391649, electronics8020142} and failure prediction\cite{9838772, 9814507} at mmWave frequencies, these techniques have not been unified into a proactive, ``just-in-time'' algorithm validated on sub-THz hardware. To bridge this gap, we introduce the PanicPacket algorithm and the MobileTHz platform. PanicPacket fuses RF and IMU data to preemptively realign the beam before failure occurs. We benchmark it against a conventional Blind Search and a reactive IMU-assisted algorithm using MobileTHz's Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) capabilities. Our results show that PanicPacket significantly improves capacity, stability, and link availability, establishing the feasibility of stable, mobile sub-THz networks.

\section*{Vision of the PanicPacket Algorithm}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/vision_PanicPacket/panicpacketvision.drawio.pdf}
\vspace{-15 pt}
\caption{PanicPacket algorithm overview. The UE detects imminent misalignment via IMU data, transmits kinematic information before exiting the Edge of Coverage, and enables preemptive AP beam realignment}
\label{fig_alignment_pdf}
\vspace{-5pt}
\end{figure}

Conventional beam management strategies typically operate retroactively, initiating recovery procedures only after signal degradation or link failure is detected\cite{Giordani2018ATO, Wang2022}. This approach is challenging in sub-THz networks due to the reliance on high-gain antennas\cite{Kleine-Ostmann2011}. While essential for closing the link budget, narrow beams create mobility constraints: they quadratically expand the spatial search space while shrinking the beam residence time (the brief window where a moving node stays aligned).

The PanicPacket algorithm bridges this gap by leveraging IMU sensor data on a mobile User Equipment (UE) to provide the Access Point (AP) with real-time motion awareness. The algorithm utilizes RF and IMU data to detect imminent beam failure by identifying when physical motion exceeds an antenna's Edge of Coverage (EOC). It shares this kinematic information between nodes before the connection breaks. This data transfer, a~\emph{PanicPacket}, allows the system to coordinate a preemptive realignment to prevent failure or facilitate a deterministic recovery if the link is severed. The algorithm leverages the sub-THz spectrum to transmit packets with microsecond-level latency, allowing the system to react to mobility events within the beam residence time.

To validate this vision, we implemented the proposed solution using the MobileTHz platform, encompassing both a high-fidelity software simulator and a hardware platform (detailed in the \textit{Methods} section). We then performed a comparative study, benchmarking the performance of the PanicPacket algorithm against two conventional algorithms: a reactive Blind Search algorithm and a reactive IMU-Assisted algorithm. The details of the study, including experimental assumptions and system parameters, are summarized in \textit{Methods}, while the key results of our evaluation are discussed in the \textit{Results} section.

\section*{Results}

We benchmark PanicPacket against a Blind Search and an IMU-Assisted algorithm, with a static (no steering) baseline and a theoretical maximum (perfect alignment) for reference. Performance is quantified through Mean Data Rate, Capacity Stability, Link Availability, and Signaling Overhead (defined in Methods).Each experiment isolates one variable: translational acceleration, rotational acceleration, or link geometry. A final study then evaluates the impact of antenna directivity under combined motion. Results are evaluated across three steering models (Table~\ref{tab:steering_and_hardware}): \textit{Constrained Mechanical}, which mirrors our laboratory hardware and anchors HIL validation; \textit{Ideal Mechanical}, representing the upper bound of mechanical actuation for use cases such as backhaul and UAV links; and \textit{Electronic}, representing electronic phased arrays for future THz access networks.

Figure~\ref{fig:trans_exp_rate} shows Mean Capacity versus Peak Acceleration for the Constrained Mechanical model, where slow mechanical steering imposes the harshest penalty on every realignment attempt. Under these constraints, the Blind Search algorithm frequently fails to complete its scan before the UE moves away, causing it to perform \textit{worse} than a static, non-steered link by 5--7\% across the acceleration range. The IMU-Assist algorithm mitigates this by narrowing the angular search space using on-device motion estimates, improving mean capacity over the Blind Search by up to 92\% at \SI{20}{m/s^2}. PanicPacket further compounds this advantage by preemptively sharing kinematics with the AP, achieving up to an 88\% improvement over IMU-Assist at low accelerations. At the highest mobility, IMU-Assist and PanicPacket reach 50\% and 69\% of the theoretical maximum, respectively, confirming that sensor-informed algorithms can make mechanically constrained hardware viable for mobile links where a purely reactive approach cannot.

To validate these simulation results, we conducted Hardware-in-the-Loop experiments across all four algorithm configurations. The experimental data closely tracked the simulation, with Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) values of 1.80--3.13\% and Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE) of 2.99--3.82\%, confirming that the MobileTHz engine captures the physical constraints of the steering hardware. The subsequent analysis therefore uses simulation to explore steering models beyond our laboratory equipment.

Figure \ref{fig:trans_sim_rate} uses the Ideal Mechanical model, where faster rotary movements impose less opportunity cost compared to the Constrained Mechanical model. The Blind Search now outperforms the static reference by at least 75\%, since the faster hardware can complete enough scan iterations within the beam residence time to recover alignment. IMU-Assist provides further gains of up to 39\% at \SI{20}{m/s^2}, though it slightly underperforms at \SI{0.5}{m/s^2} due to overshooting from aggressive motion prediction (discussed in Methods). PanicPacket delivers the largest improvement across the full range, narrowing the gap to the theoretical maximum by synchronizing both nodes rather than relying on the AP's reactive scan alone. This result demonstrates that even when hardware is fast enough for reactive recovery to succeed, proactive coordination still yields substantial gains by eliminating unnecessary search overhead.

With Electronic (Figure~\ref{fig:trans_simfast_rate}), where steering latency is negligible, the performance spread compresses: Blind Search converges at \SI{55.3}{Gbps}, IMU-Assist at \SI{63.4}{Gbps}, and PanicPacket at \SI{72.6}{Gbps}. Yet PanicPacket still retains a 12\% advantage over IMU-Assist and 30\% over Blind Search, showing that the search-space penalty of reactive methods persists even when hardware is no longer the bottleneck.

Although mean capacity provides theoretical maximum data rates, it masks temporal variance; thus, it is also important to evaluate capacity variance for mobile link robustness. Figure~\ref{fig:trans_metrics_example} demonstrates a single mobility event, showcasing the power variances over time. The Blind Search algorithm experiences the most power oscillations during movement, while the IMU-Assist algorithm decreases temporal variance as well as the magnitude of link loss. PanicPacket outperforms the other algorithms, providing a stable link and the highest final received power of \SI{-12.7}{dBm}.

Figure~\ref{fig:trans_metrics_covariance} quantifies the coefficient of variation (CoV) of the capacity across the three algorithms as peak acceleration increases under the Ideal Mechanical model. The IMU-Assist algorithm reduces CoV by up to 66\% compared to the Blind Search, demonstrating that IMU integration improves not only mean capacity (as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:trans_sim_rate}, where IMU-Assist achieved up to a 39\% increase) but also link stability. However, single-node motion compensation alone is insufficient to achieve a consistently low-variance link. PanicPacket shows that stability can be further improved when both nodes share mobility awareness, reducing CoV by up to 89.2\% compared to Blind Search and by 23.7\% compared to IMU-Assist.

Overhead, defined as the potential channel capacity consumed by PanicPacket transmission, is quantified in Figure~\ref{fig:trans_metrics_overhead}. Overhead increases to \SI{1.3}{m/s^2}, the point at which the UE consistently moves beyond the AP's EOC causing more frequent alignment. At higher accelerations, however, overhead decreases as the AP's look-ahead strategy results in fewer intermediate realignments before the UE reaches its final position. Overall, PanicPacket improves mean capacity and reduces link variance while consuming at most 0.26\% of the total channel capacity, demonstrating its efficiency in high-mobility regimes.

Rotational motion often presents the greatest challenge to beam management, as typical angular accelerations can be significantly higher than the angular rates induced by translational movement~\cite{9391649} (see also Appendix, Figures~\ref{fig:imu_hand_motion} and~\ref{fig:imu_body_rotation}). Unlike translation, where the geometric Line-of-Sight (LoS) angle changes at both nodes and both must steer to compensate, pure rotation alters only the UE's orientation and the AP's LoS remains unchanged. The correct response is therefore for the UE to counter-rotate while the AP holds its position. Reactive algorithms, relying solely on RF power drops, cannot distinguish rotation from translation and default to searching at both nodes. Under pure rotation, this causes the AP to actively steer \emph{away} from the correct direction, turning the recovery mechanism itself into a source of misalignment.

This behavior is quantified in Figure~\ref{fig:rot_sim_rate}, where the Blind Search's capacity degrades to \SI{5}{}--\SI{6}{Gbps}, consistently falling below the static reference across the full acceleration range. The IMU-Assist algorithm breaks this failure at one node: the UE uses on-device sensor data to identify the motion as rotation and counter-rotates to maintain alignment rather than initiating a scan, recovering capacity to approximately \SI{30}{Gbps}. However, this correction is local to the UE. The AP, unaware that the UE has already compensated, may still detect a transient power drop and trigger its own reactive scan, partially undoing the UE's correction. This coordination gap caps IMU-Assist at around 36.5\% of the theoretical maximum. PanicPacket closes this gap, sustaining capacity above \SI{55}{Gbps} up to \SI{120}{deg/s^2}. Even at \SI{300}{deg/s^2}, representative of upper-range human rotational acceleration (see Appendix, Figures \ref{fig:imu_hand_motion} and~\ref{fig:imu_body_rotation}), PanicPacket maintains a 47\% advantage over IMU-Assist.

The Electronic model (Figure~\ref{fig:rot_simfast_rate}) also demonstrates the Blind Search remains below the static reference, since fast steering cannot correct a misdirected search. IMU-Assist benefits substantially, settling around \SI{38}{Gbps} at \SI{700}{deg/s^2}, while PanicPacket sustains above \SI{57}{Gbps} across the full range by ensuring the AP holds position rather than scanning.

Under experimental constraints (Figure~\ref{fig:rot_exp_rate}), all algorithms degrade more rapidly than in the idealized scenarios because the rotary stages cannot complete the required angular correction within the beam residence time. Blind Search remains at \SI{7}{}--\SI{8}{Gbps} throughout, consistently below the static reference. IMU-Assist falls from \SI{43}{Gbps} to under \SI{10}{Gbps} by \SI{100}{deg/s^2}, while PanicPacket drops from \SI{60}{Gbps} to \SI{30}{Gbps} over the same interval, retaining a $3\times$ capacity advantage that demonstrates proactive coordination can extend the usable rotational range of mechanically steered systems well beyond what reactive algorithms support. Experimental results track simulation with MAPE of 3.88--11.66\% and NRMSE of 5.19--17.5\%, with the higher errors concentrated in the Blind Search where low absolute capacities amplify small deviations. PanicPacket and IMU-Assist show closer agreement (MAPE of 3.88\% and 4.68\%), validating the MobileTHz engine under rotational dynamics.

The reactive methods, by contrast, stay flat at 25--40\% regardless of acceleration, as their search cannot converge before the UE exits the beam.

\section*{Discussion}

The above results characterize mobile sub-THz link performance as a function of link geometry, motion type and severity, and beam steering hardware, comparing four beam realignment strategies of increasing sophistication: a static baseline, a reactive Blind Search, a reactive IMU-Assisted approach in line with prior work at lower frequencies~\cite{s141019622, Brambilla_2019}, and the proposed proactive PanicPacket algorithm. \emph{To our knowledge, this constitutes one of the first comprehensive studies of a hybrid proactive and reactive beam realignment technique for mobile sub-THz communications validated on an end-to-end hardware prototype.} The results, corroborated by both simulation and hardware-in-the-loop measurements, lead to several principal conclusions.

The \emph{first} key conclusion is that building a reliable and high-performance truly mobile sub-THz communication system demands an upgrade from the state-of-the-art \emph{reactive} beam management procedures to novel \emph{proactive} beam management solutions. Specifically, the results in Fig.~\ref{fig:accelLinkAvail} illustrate that the link availability for the reactive ``Blind Search'' solution never raises above $40\%$ for moderate-to-low mobility levels. Hence, such an unreliable communication link cannot be utilized in practice for any latency-sensitive or reliability-sensitive (e.g., multimedia) services envisioned for mobile sub-THz communications. In contrast, the proposed proactive IMU-assisted ``PanicPacket'' solution demonstrates superior performance here, achieving up to $90+\%$ (sometimes, up to $100\%$) link availability in the same conditions.

The \emph{second} key conclusion from the present study is that the sole presence of an IMU at a mobile node and the use of IMU data just in the beam realignment procedure (as discussed in several prior works for lower frequencies, including microwave and mmWave) is, still insufficient for mobile sub-THz. While the results in Fig.~\ref{fig:translational_rate}--Fig.~\ref{fig:angle_effects}, in general, demonstrate superior performance and reliability levels of the implemented ``IMU-Assisted'' solution over the ``Blind Search'' one, the absolute levels of link availability for ``IMU-Assisted'' are still notably lower than the desired levels achievable with the proposed proactive beam realignment approach (``PanicPacket''). This contrast is particularly visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:antenna_comparison} presenting the link availability for different acceleration levels and different antenna configurations. While the proactive ``PanicPacket'' solution achieves a relatively wide range or operation with over $80\%$ of link availability even with the narrowest-beam antenna with just \SI{1.3}{\degree} HPBW, the highest point for the ``IMU-Assisted'' is twice lower (around $40\%$) and the average point is 3--4 times lower (notably under $20\%$). Hence, we may conclude that for mobile sub-THz communications featuring narrow-beam antennas, not every IMU-assisted beam realignment is sufficient, but only the proactive one (that utilizes the IMU data to continuously improve the communication link quality, not only in the event that the link gets interrupted).

Notably, as further confirmed by our study, while the proactive ``PanicPacket'' beam alignment approach introduces a control signaling overhead, the penalty brought by this overhead is minimal compared to the gains in capacity and reliability. Our results further indicate that the signaling overhead scales linearly with the rotation angle, increasing from near \SI{0}{\percent} to a maximum of only \SI{0.3}{\percent} of the total link capacity (Fig.~\ref{fig:angle_overhead}). On a link providing tens of gigabits per second, this overhead equates to only hundreds of kilobits per second, a negligible bandwidth penalty while providing significant improvements in mean capacity and link availability. In summary, the additional control data required for the ``PanicPacket'' protocol may be sent with negligible signaling overhead, thus not decreasing the link capacity during the data exchange phase.

The \emph{third} key conclusion from our performed study is that the choice of the particular beam steering hardware platform (e.g., different mechanical beam steering vs. electronic beam steering solutions) is equally if not more important than the choice of the particular beam re-alignment algorithms and protocols. Specifically, while the proposed ``PanicPacket'' protocol has the ability to minimize the impact of hardware constraints, particularly in latency-bound scenarios, the average capacity results in Fig.~\ref{fig:translational_rate} differ substantially depending on the selected beam steering method: state-of-the-art mechanical (mimicking our hardware test bed), ideal mechanical, or electronic. Nevertheless, the proposed ``PanicPacket'' solution effectively mitigates these latency limitations by decoupling failure prevention from failure recovery. By utilizing a just-in-time trigger based on sensor fusion, the system maintains a consistent link stability, reflected in a CoV of approximately 0.1 as observed in the translational mobility analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:trans_metrics_covariance}). This stability is critical for transport-layer protocols like TCP that are sensitive to packet loss and jitter\cite{10.1145/52325.52356}. 

Our \emph{fourth and final} major conclusion from the performed study is that the design of simultaneously high-performance, robust, and truly mobile sub-THz nodes requires a shift in antenna selection approach. While traditional designs for stationary sub-THz connectivity systems prioritize maximizing gain to overcome path loss and achieve the highest peak SNR values, our results indicate that mobility introduces a competing constraint: the beamwidth-latency product. Although higher gain antennas improve SNR, their narrower beamwidths naturally reduce the system's tolerance for pointing errors and latency. In our study, the \SI{13}{\degree} antenna suffered from insufficient gain to maintain the link during transient slewing events, while the \SI{4}{\degree} and \SI{1.3}{\degree} antennas failed because the angular displacement during the reaction time exceeded their beamwidths. For our particular configuration, we identified a "Goldilocks" zone—represented by the \SI{9}{\degree} antenna—that balanced gain margin with alignment tolerance. Although these specific parameters are unique to our experimental hardware, the broader implication is that antenna selection must account for the platform's specific mobility profile and control latency. Here, the MobileTHz engine developed in this work also serves as a useful solution to characterize this antenna-related trade-off. It particularly allows communication engineers to identify the optimal antenna parameters required to support different classes of movement for future truly mobile sub-THz communication systems.

\emph{Finally}, several avenues remain for extending PanicPacket toward a more intelligent and robust mechanism. The current hard-coded rule-based realignment could be better tailored to specific environments using learning-based decision making~\cite{10036372}, whether AI-driven or based on advanced heuristics. A related limitation is the LoS assumption: the present evaluation does not consider transient blockage events (e.g., a hand or body obstructing the beam), which represent a complementary failure mode to misalignment. However, the PanicPacket framework offers a natural path toward blockage discrimination. If PanicPackets were transmitted periodically during steady-state operation rather than only at the Edge of Coverage boundary, the AP would receive continuous kinematic updates. A sudden RF power drop \emph{without} a corresponding change in the UE's reported motion state would indicate an external obstruction rather than misalignment, enabling the AP to suppress unnecessary beam searches and instead await blockage clearance or initiate a handover. We note that current sub-THz link budgets offer limited margin even under LoS conditions, and the substantial penetration losses introduced by common indoor materials at sub-THz frequencies~\cite{9473898} make NLoS paths insufficient to reliably sustain the ultra-high data rates targeted by 6G systems, making LoS misalignment mitigation the immediate priority. The present study is also limited to a single AP--UE link; in a multi-user deployment, the per-user kinematic data carried by PanicPackets could enable mobility-aware beam scheduling and more favorable scaling of aggregate steering overhead compared to reactive methods. Quantifying these multi-user gains, exploring the periodic reporting mode and its interaction with blockage-aware protocols, and extending the framework to NLoS-aware operation remain important directions for future work.

The MobileTHz platform comprises a modular C++ simulation engine and a Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) experimental framework for testing beam management algorithms in high-mobility sub-THz scenarios. \subsection*{Blind Search Reactive Search}
\begin{wrapfigure}{R}{0.4\linewidth}
    \centering
    \vspace{-15 pt}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Methods/algorithm_baseline/baseline.pdf}
    \vspace{-15 pt}
    \caption{Blind Search spiral trajectory, scanning outward from the last known-good direction (center). Blue arrows trace the discrete spiral path; the dark-to-light gradient indicates time progression.}
    \vspace{-5pt}
    \label{fig:blind_spiral_search}
\end{wrapfigure}
The Blind Search initiates when a short-term Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) of received power drops below a beamwidth-dependent threshold (parameters in Table~\ref{tab:system_parameters}), indicating link failure. Upon detection, the AP executes an discrete spiral search adapted from optical systems~\cite{photonics11060540}, where the step size expands exponentially to account for growing positional uncertainty. The step size for successive rings, $S_k$, grows from an initial value proportional to the antenna's HPBW:
\begin{equation}
    S_k = S_0 \cdot \gamma^k,
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the ring index, $S_0 = \theta_{\text{HPBW}}/2$ is the initial step size (half the antenna beamwidth), and $\gamma = 1.25$ is the growth factor. The search terminates when received power exceeds a time-relaxing threshold $P_{succ}(\Delta t)$, defined relative to the last known-good reference power $P_{ref}$:
\begin{equation}
    P_{succ}(\Delta t) = P_{ref} - \Gamma(\Delta t) = P_{ref} - (\Gamma_{0} + R \cdot \Delta t),
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{0}$ is the initial margin, $R$ is the margin increase rate, and $\Delta t$ is the elapsed time since failure detection. Once the link is re-established, a final peak-seeking phase fine-tunes alignment to the local power maximum.

\subsection*{IMU-Assisted Reactive Search}
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\linewidth}
    \centering
    \vspace{-15 pt}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Methods/algorithm_IMUreact/imu.pdf}
    \vspace{-15 pt}
    \caption{IMU-Assisted search trajectory. Green arrows show IMU-derived compensation shifts applied before each spiral step, relocating the search center to the sensor-predicted LoS direction. Blue arrows trace the tight local spiral.}
    \label{fig:imu_local_search}
    \vspace{-5pt}
\end{wrapfigure}
The IMU-Assisted algorithm uses the same power-drop trigger as the Blind Search but replaces the blind spiral with a sensor-directed recovery, conceptually similar to Kalman-filter-based predictive tracking~\cite{8455289,9145366} but operating in a triggered rather than continuous mode. Upon failure detection, the UE integrates accelerometer data into a displacement vector $\Delta\mathbf{p}(t)$ and fuses gyroscope data for orientation change, predicting the new LoS via:
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\text{az}}^{\star}(t) = \theta_{\text{az,0}} + \arctan\!\left(\frac{\Delta p_{y}}{d_{\text{link}}}\right) - \Delta\psi(t)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\text{el}}^{\star}(t) = \theta_{\text{el,0}} + \arctan\!\left(\frac{\Delta p_{z}}{d_{\text{link}}}\right) + \Delta\phi(t)
\end{equation}
where $\theta_{\text{az,0}}$ and $\theta_{\text{el,0}}$ are the last known-good angles; $\Delta p_y$ and $\Delta p_z$ are the horizontal and vertical components of displacement perpendicular to the LoS; $d_{\text{link}}$ is the AP--UE link distance, evaluated at the last known-good reference time; and $\Delta\psi(t)$ and $\Delta\phi(t)$ are the changes in yaw and pitch, respectively. The UE then steers directly to this predicted angle. Because IMU integration accumulates drift, the algorithm follows the predicted steer with a tight spiral search at step size:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{local}} = \beta \cdot \theta_{\text{HPBW}}, \quad (\beta = 0.1)
\end{equation}
This localized search corrects for residual sensor error and settles on the precise power peak.

\subsection*{PanicPacket Algorithm}
PanicPacket preempts link failure by separating prevention from recovery. A stationary Zero Velocity Update (ZUPT) phase first calibrates accelerometer bias $\mathbf{b}_a$ and gyroscope bias $\mathbf{b}_\omega$, enabling the system to distinguish link-critical motion from sensor noise. The UE then continuously computes a ``Link Deviation Velocity'' $\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}$, which unifies rotational and LoS-perpendicular translational motion into a single angular rate of deviation:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}} = \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{UE}} + \frac{\mathbf{v}_{\perp}}{d_{\text{link}}},
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{UE}}$ is the UE's angular velocity, $\mathbf{v}_{\perp}$ is its translational velocity perpendicular to the LoS, and $d_{\text{link}}$ is the instantaneous link distance. A `PanicPacket` is triggered when the projected angular displacement over the system's reaction time $T_{\text{react}}$ exceeds a fraction $\eta$ of the antenna's HPBW:
\begin{equation}
    \|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\| \cdot T_{\text{react}} > \eta \cdot \theta_{\text{HPBW}}
\end{equation}
This ensures the system reacts only when the combination of motion speed and link geometry threatens to move the UE beyond the AP's beam before the next control cycle. Upon triggering, the UE transmits a control frame containing its integrated displacement vector and a buffer of recent IMU samples. The AP applies an EWMA to the buffered samples to derive a filtered acceleration trend $\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{\text{predict}}$, extrapolating the UE's position at steering completion. By steering to this predicted future location rather than the last known position, the system maintains SNR continuity despite rapid motion.

Each control frame follows the IEEE~802.15.3d THz-SC structure~\cite{9269931}, using a short Preamble Acquisition Preamble (PAP) of \SI{1.89}{\micro\second} and the minimum 2048-byte frame payload. With worst-case BPSK modulation and 11/15 LDPC coding, each frame occupies approximately $T_{\text{frame}} \approx \SI{5}{\micro\second}$. Because the UE must first transmit the control frame and then the AP must process it before issuing a steering command, the end-to-end reaction latency is $T_{\text{react}} = 2 \cdot T_{\text{frame}} = \SI{10}{\micro\second}$. Reliable decoding of the control frame at the AP requires a minimum receive SNR of \SI{9.59}{\deci\bel}, corresponding to BPSK with 11/15 LDPC coding at a target $\text{BER} = 10^{-3}$. This threshold determines the maximum range at which PanicPacket signaling remains operational: if the instantaneous SNR falls below \SI{9.59}{\deci\bel}, the AP cannot decode the control frame and the system falls back to reactive recovery.

A mobile scenario consists of a set of mobile nodes, $\mathcal{M} = \{m_1, m_2, \dots\}$, whose trajectories are specified by a sequence of keyframes, $\{(\mathbf{p}_k, \mathbf{q}_k)\}_{k=0}^{K-1}$. Each keyframe defines the position $\mathbf{p}_k \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and orientation (as a unit quaternion) $\mathbf{q}_k \in \mathbb{S}^3$ at a specific time slot $n_k$. To create smooth and realistic translational paths between keyframes, the platform interpolates position using Perlin's smootherstep function, $f(\tau)$:
\begin{equation}
f(\tau) = 6\tau^5 - 15\tau^4 + 10\tau^3,
\label{eq:smootherstep}
\end{equation}
where $\tau = (t - t_k) / (t_{k+1} - t_k) \in [0, 1]$ is the normalized time. This function's $C^2$ continuity guarantees zero velocity, acceleration, and jerk at keyframe boundaries, producing physically plausible motion. The resulting maximum acceleration for a displacement $D$ over duration $T$ is:
\begin{equation}
    a_{\max} = K \cdot \frac{D}{T^2},
\end{equation}
where $K \approx 5.7735$ is derived from the peak of the second derivative of Eq.~\eqref{eq:smootherstep}. Orientation, in turn, is interpolated via Spherical Linear Interpolation (SLERP):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Slerp}(\mathbf{q}_k, \mathbf{q}_{k+1}, \tau) = \frac{\sin((1-\tau)\Theta)}{\sin\Theta}\mathbf{q}_k + \frac{\sin(\tau\Theta)}{\sin\Theta}\mathbf{q}_{k+1},
\end{equation}
where $\Theta = \arccos(\mathbf{q}_k \cdot \mathbf{q}_{k+1})$. Linear and angular velocities and accelerations are obtained via central finite differences of position and orientation, respectively.

Given the updated node kinematics, the engine evaluates the RF link state. Free-space path loss (FSPL) in dB is:
\begin{equation}
    L_{p} = 20 \log_{10}(d_{\text{link}}) + 20 \log_{10}(f_c) - 147.55,
\end{equation}
where $d_{\text{link}} = ||\mathbf{p}_{rx} - \mathbf{p}_{tx}||$ is the instantaneous LoS distance in meters and $f_c$ is the carrier frequency in Hz. Antenna directivity is evaluated from the node's effective orientation $\mathbf{q}_{eff} = \mathbf{q}_{body} \otimes \mathbf{q}_{rotary}$, which transforms the LoS vector into the antenna's local frame to obtain the off-axis angle. The directivity follows the E-plane pattern of a WR-6.5 conical horn, whose gain $G(\theta)$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    G(\theta) = \frac{1 + \cos(\theta)}{2} \sqrt{\frac{(C(r_2) - C(r_1))^2 + (S(r_2) - S(r_1))^2}{4(C(r_0)^2 + S(r_0)^2)}},
\end{equation}
where $C(x)$ and $S(x)$ are the Fresnel cosine and sine integrals, respectively, and $r_0$, $r_1$, and $r_2$ are functions of the horn aperture radius, slant length, and observation angle $\theta$. The received power (in dBm) then combines transmit power, directional gains, and path loss:
\begin{equation}
P_{rx} = P_{tx} + G_{tx}(\theta_{tx}) + G_{rx}(\theta_{rx}) - L_p,
\end{equation}
where $P_{tx}$ is the transmit power and $\theta_{tx}$, $\theta_{rx}$ are the off-axis angles at the transmitter and receiver, respectively. To account for small-scale fading, the final received power in linear scale, $p_{rx} = 10^{P_{rx}/10}$~(mW), is modeled as an exponentially distributed random variable whose mean is the ideal (unfaded) linear received power.

The simulation then applies a hardware abstraction layer that models the constraints of physical components. A steerable antenna's orientation is controlled by a dual-axis mechanical stage, which adjusts the $\mathbf{q}_{rotary}$ component of the effective orientation. For any instructed angular displacement $\Delta\theta$, the model uses a trapezoidal velocity profile:
\begin{equation}
	a(t) =
	\begin{cases}
		+\alpha_{\text{max}} & \text{Phase I: Acceleration}       \\
		0               & \text{Phase II: Constant velocity} \\
		-\alpha_{\text{max}} & \text{Phase III: Deceleration}     \\
	\end{cases},
\end{equation}
where the duration of each phase is determined by $\Delta\theta$ and the stage's maximum angular velocity ($\omega_{max}$) and angular acceleration ($\alpha_{max}$) constraints.

Beyond mechanical steering, the hardware abstraction layer captures two additional sources of latency and noise. A configurable processing delay $T_{\text{proc}}$ models signal acquisition and digital conversion latency, holding the power reading constant at the value sampled at the start of the previous processing interval. The simulated IMU provides gyroscope ($\boldsymbol{\omega}$), accelerometer ($\mathbf{f}$), and magnetometer data, with realistic outputs generated by corrupting ground-truth kinematics with bias and noise. Denoting the discrete time-slot index by $n$, the simulated accelerometer output $\tilde{\mathbf{a}}[n]$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{a}}[n] = \mathbf{f}_{ideal}[n] + \mathbf{b}_{a}[n] + \mathbf{n}_{a}[n].
\end{equation}
The bias $\mathbf{b}_{a}[n]$ evolves as a random walk: $\mathbf{b}_{a}[n] = \mathbf{b}_{a}[n-1] + \mathbf{w}_{b,a}[n]$, where the update term $\mathbf{w}_{b,a} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{a,b}^2 \Delta t_{\text{sim}} \cdot \mathbf{I}_3)$ and the white noise is $\mathbf{n}_{a}[n] \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (\sigma_{a,n}^2 / \Delta t_{\text{sim}}) \cdot \mathbf{I}_3)$. A similar noise model is applied to the gyroscope readings. The noise density parameters $\sigma_{a,n}$ and bias random walk parameter $\sigma_{a,b}$ correspond to the Accelerometer Noise Density and Initial Bias values in Table~\ref{tab:system_parameters}, respectively.

This sequence of kinematics update, channel evaluation, hardware emulation, and algorithm decision repeats at every time slot, producing the continuous per-slot feedback loop that drives all simulated results in this work.

The same per-slot loop executes in HIL mode, but with simulated RF and sensor inputs replaced by physical measurements: received power from the RF hardware, orientation from rotary-stage encoders, and inertial data from an on-device IMU. The RF chain operates in the WR-6.5 waveguide band (110--170~GHz). An in-house C++ library (RotaryLib) commands the rotary tables via serial connection, executing timed movement profiles that replicate the trapezoidal velocity model used in simulation. Power measurements, encoder feedback, and IMU samples are streamed back to the MobileTHz engine, allowing the beam management algorithms to operate identically on real-time physical data.

\subsection*{Evaluation Metrics}
We evaluate the proposed beam management algorithms using four Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) derived from the RF and mobility parameters outlined in Tables~\ref{tab:system_parameters} and~\ref{tab:steering_and_hardware}. Each simulation curve in Figures~\ref{fig:translational_rate}--\ref{fig:antenna_comparison} averages 5{,}000 Monte Carlo runs with uniformly randomized UE movement direction. The experimental markers in Figures~\ref{fig:trans_exp_rate} and~\ref{fig:rot_exp_rate} each average 10 HIL trials, constrained to the azimuth plane by the weight of the RF front-ends.

Mean Capacity serves as the primary metric for spectral efficiency, calculated as the time-average of the instantaneous Shannon capacity $C(t)$ over the scenario duration $T$:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{C} = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}(t)) \, dt
\end{equation}
where $B$ represents the channel bandwidth. However, mean capacity obscures the impact of beam misalignment and outages on higher-layer protocols (e.g., TCP). To quantify link stability, we compute the Capacity Coefficient of Variation (CoV), normalizing the standard deviation of capacity ($\sigma_C$) by its mean ($\mu_C$):
\begin{equation}
    \text{CoV} = \frac{\sigma_C}{\mu_C}
\end{equation}
High CoV values indicate erratic tracking and jitter, whereas values approaching zero indicate a stable and reliable link.

To assess the operational feasibility of the link for data transmission, we evaluate Link Availability ($A_{\text{link}}$). This metric shows when the link budget is sufficient to support high-order modulation schemes, specifically where the $\text{SNR}(t)$ exceeds a critical threshold $\text{SNR}_{\text{th}}$:

\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{link}} = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} \mathbbm{1}\!\left(\text{SNR}(t) \geq \text{SNR}_{\text{th}}\right) dt \times 100\%
\end{equation}

The threshold $\text{SNR}_{\text{th}} = \SI{20}{\deci\bel}$ corresponds to 64-QAM at a FEC $\text{BER} = 10^{-3}$, derived from the standard $M$-QAM approximation:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR}_{\text{th}} \approx \frac{M-1}{3} \left[ Q^{-1} \left( \frac{\text{BER}_{\text{target}} \cdot \log_2 M}{4} \right) \right]^2
\end{equation}
which evaluates to \SI{18.8}{\deci\bel}.

Finally, we quantify the bandwidth cost of PanicPacket signaling via Signaling Overhead ($O_{\text{sig}}$). During each control frame transmission the channel cannot carry user data, so the foregone capacity over $N_{\text{pp}}$ PanicPacket events is:
\begin{equation}
    L_{\text{ctrl}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{pp}}} C(t_i) \cdot T_{\text{frame}},
\end{equation}
where $C(t_i)$ is the instantaneous capacity at the $i$-th transmission and $T_{\text{frame}}$ is the control frame duration derived in the PanicPacket Algorithm subsection. Signaling overhead is then:
\begin{equation}
    O_{\text{sig}} = \frac{L_{\text{ctrl}}}{\int_{0}^{T} C(t) \, dt} \times 100\%
\end{equation}
