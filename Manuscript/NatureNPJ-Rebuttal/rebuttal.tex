\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{Clearly differentiate your pre-failure trigger and coordinated AP/UE mechanism from prior predictive tracking and IMU-assisted beam management. Provide a more detailed related-work comparison beyond the two baselines.}
}
\end{tcbremark}

\noindent We have expanded the related-work positioning in the revised manuscript. The two reactive baselines (Hierarchical Search and IMU-Assist) remain the closest constructive comparators that share JIT's protocol-level scope, but we now contrast JIT explicitly against four additional families of prior work: (i)~predictive RF-only tracking using sub-6\,GHz channel features~\cite{Alrabeiah2020DeepLearning}, (ii)~infrastructure-side multimodal sensing with LiDAR, radar, vision, and GPS~\cite{Alkhateeb2023DeepSense, Jiang2023LiDAR, Morais2023Position}, (iii)~orientation-conditioned UE-side beam selection, including IMU-driven head-mounted-display beamforming~\cite{Rezaie2022Orientation, Struye2023CoVRage}, and (iv)~bilateral pilot-driven coordination consistent with 3GPP Rel-18 BM-Case~\cite{Alkhateeb2018DLCB, Xue2023Standardization, Heng2021SixChallenges}.\\

\noindent What is specifically new in JIT is the combination of three elements: (i)~UE-side IMU motion sensing, (ii)~a pre-failure trigger that anticipates the next beam-edge crossing, and (iii)~coordinated AP/UE realignment via a single low-overhead control frame. To our knowledge, no prior work combines all three within a single sub-THz protocol with hardware-in-the-loop validation. Concretely: (i)~predictive sub-6\,GHz–to-mmWave approaches~\cite{Alrabeiah2020DeepLearning} need a continuous RF reference; (ii)~multimodal AP-side sensing~\cite{Alkhateeb2023DeepSense, Jiang2023LiDAR, Morais2023Position} adds infrastructure hardware (LiDAR, radar, cameras) and raises regulatory and privacy questions, whereas IMUs are already in most modern smartphones; (iii)~orientation-aided beam selection~\cite{Rezaie2022Orientation, Struye2023CoVRage} uses orientation only as a one-shot codebook lookup, not to anticipate the next crossing; and (iv)~pilot-driven coordination~\cite{Alkhateeb2018DLCB, Xue2023Standardization} relies on continuous pilot exchanges that consume RF airtime regardless of whether the link is at risk, whereas JIT sends a single control frame only when a crossing is imminent (overhead $<0.4\%$).\\

\noindent The corresponding manuscript change is a new related-work paragraph at the end of the \emph{Introduction} covering the four families above, with per-family contrast on sensor / modality, band, pre-failure triggering, coordinated AP+UE realignment, and validation scope, and a closing statement that no prior approach jointly delivers UE-side IMU sensing, a pre-failure trigger, and coordinated AP/UE realignment over a sub-THz protocol with hardware-in-the-loop validation.

\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{Analyze robustness of the trigger under long-term IMU drift without zero-velocity updates, and explain the effect of the 10\,ms IMU sampling period on sub-millisecond control reaction.}

\noindent A new dedicated \emph{IMU Drift and Sampling-Rate Considerations} subsubsection has been added inside \emph{Methods/JIT Beam Realignment}, addressing both points below.\\

\noindent First, on long-term drift. The UE re-evaluates Eq.~(7) at every IMU sample, but each evaluation only projects the current Link Deviation Velocity forward by $T_{\text{react}} \approx \SI{15}{\micro\second}$. The prediction horizon relevant to one trigger decision is therefore $T_{\text{react}}$, not the full scenario duration. For accelerometer white noise of one-sided density $\sigma_{a,n}$, the standard random-walk position drift over $T_{\text{react}}$ is
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_p = \sigma_{a,n}\sqrt{\frac{T_{\text{react}}^{3}}{3}}.
\end{equation}
With $\sigma_{a,n} = \SI{0.0294}{\meter\per\second\squared\per\sqrt{\hertz}}$ from Table~2 and $T_{\text{react}} \approx \SI{15}{\micro\second}$, this gives $\sigma_{p} \lesssim \SI{1}{\nano\meter}$, an angular drift of order $10^{-8}$~degrees at $d_{\text{link}} = \SI{3}{\meter}$, many orders of magnitude below $\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$.\\

\noindent Two further design choices keep bias drift from accumulating across longer sessions. The trigger uses the Link Deviation \emph{velocity} (single accelerometer integration, drift grows linearly in time), not absolute position (double integration, quadratic). And the control frame transmits only the \emph{displacement delta since the last transmission}, with the accumulator reset at every transmission (cooldown $\approx \SI{50}{\milli\second}$); the drift the AP ever sees is therefore bounded by this short inter-transmission interval. The initial stationary ZUPT at startup is still required to calibrate the IMU bias before any motion is integrated; what becomes unnecessary at JIT's timescales is \emph{periodic re-ZUPT during continuous operation}. For longer-mobility deployments, an opportunistic ZUPT can refresh the bias whenever $\|\dot{\boldsymbol\delta}\|$ remains below a low-motion threshold for several control cycles, without interrupting data exchange. \todo{do we need to back up this opportunistic-ZUPT claim with data, or is the design-level description enough? not sure -- consider before submitting.}\\

\noindent Second, the \SI{10}{\milli\second} IMU sampling period gates only the \emph{evaluation rate} of Eq.~(7); it does not bound control reaction. Once a trigger fires, the control-frame transmission ($T_{\text{frame}} \approx \SI{5}{\micro\second}$), AP processing ($T_{\text{proc}} = \SI{10}{\micro\second}$), and electronic or mechanical actuation operate on their own clocks. Hence, the IMU rate caps the trigger-detection latency (worst case, one IMU period) but does not affect how quickly the system \emph{responds} once detection has occurred. In our evaluation, this trigger-detection latency is small relative to the beam residence time at all considered motion levels, so the \SI{10}{\milli\second} sampling period does not become a bottleneck.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------%
\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{Explain how the edge of coverage is defined and whether it adapts to UE speed or rotation. Also describe how the UE detects approaching EoC to transmit IMU data.}
}
\end{tcbremark}

\noindent A new \emph{Trigger Boundary and Edge of Coverage} subsubsection has been added at the head of \emph{Methods/JIT Beam Realignment}, covering all three sub-questions.\\

\noindent The Edge of Coverage (EoC) is now formally defined as the angle off the AP's beam axis at which the antenna gain has dropped by \SI{3}{\deci\bel}, i.e., $\theta_{\text{HPBW}}/2$ off boresight. Crossing the EoC corresponds to a worst-case \SI{6}{\deci\bel} round-trip gain loss and, depending on the link budget, can directly cause link failure.\\

\noindent Because waiting until the UE reaches the EoC leaves no time to signal the AP, JIT places a \emph{trigger boundary} inside the EoC, at the angle where the projected displacement over the reaction time $T_{\text{react}}$ would reach $\eta \cdot \theta_{\text{HPBW}}$. This boundary is adaptive: when the UE moves faster (larger $\|\dot{\boldsymbol\delta}\|$), the projected displacement crosses $\eta \cdot \theta_{\text{HPBW}}$ at a smaller offset from boresight, so the trigger fires earlier, farther inside the EoC. When motion slows, the boundary expands outward toward the EoC, suppressing unnecessary control frames. The trigger therefore adapts continuously to the UE's instantaneous speed and rotation rather than being fixed.\\

\noindent For the AP-stationary scenarios considered in this work (e.g., a wall-mounted access point serving mobile UEs), detection of approaching EoC is performed \emph{locally} at the UE: at every IMU sample, the UE evaluates Eq.~(7) using its body-frame kinematics and the known $\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$ of its own antenna, requiring no AP-side feedback. While the link is still inside the beam, the UE can therefore predict the future crossing of the EoC purely from its own kinematic state and act on that prediction before the link is lost. Mobile-AP cases (e.g., UAV-borne access points) would require the AP to share its own kinematic state via the same control-frame protocol, a direct extension we leave to future work.

\end{tcbremark}

\noindent For the AP-stationary scenarios considered in this work (e.g., a wall-mounted access point serving mobile UEs), the UE detects the approaching EoC locally, without any RF feedback from the AP. At every IMU sample, the UE evaluates Eq.~(7) using its body-frame angular and translational velocities (transformed into the AP-anchored world frame via the orientation quaternion $\mathbf{q}_{\text{body}}$) and the known $\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$ of its own antenna. If the projected displacement over $T_{\text{react}}$ would exceed $\eta \cdot \theta_{\text{HPBW}}$, the UE sends a control frame. While the link is still inside the beam, the UE can therefore predict the future crossing from its own kinematic state and act before the link is lost. Mobile-AP cases (e.g., UAV-borne access points) would require the AP to share its own kinematic state via the same control-frame protocol, a direct extension we leave to future work. The new \emph{Trigger Boundary and Edge of Coverage} subsubsection describes this UE-side detection explicitly.

{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{In Figure~2, the JIT method shows trends that seem to contradict the IMU-assisted approach at high acceleration. What limitations in the JIT method explain this divergence from IMU-assisted performance? In contrast, in Figure~4, why does JIT match the IMU trend in the electronic model? \noindent Fig.~2 and Fig.~4 plot different motion types (translational vs.\ rotational), and Fig.~5 adds a different metric (link availability rather than mean capacity). The apparent inconsistency comes from this difference, not from a methodological issue.\\

\noindent Fig.~2 sweeps \emph{translational} acceleration with mean capacity. As acceleration grows, the beam residence time shrinks. IMU-Assist loses lock because its reactive search cannot converge within this shrinking window, while JIT's pre-failure trigger and AP/UE coordination preserve alignment. The capacity gap therefore widens with acceleration.\\

\noindent Fig.~4 sweeps \emph{rotational} acceleration, also with mean capacity. In the Electronic model (Fig.~4(c)), the steering hardware is fast enough that IMU-Assist's local UE counter-rotation nearly keeps up with the motion, so the relative capacity gap narrows. JIT still keeps an absolute capacity lead of about \SI{19}{Gbps} at \SI{700}{deg/s^2} because the AP holds its position rather than starting a misdirected scan. The link-availability view (Fig.~5) under the Ideal Mechanical model tells the same story: only JIT sustains a usable link across the full rotational range.\\

\noindent Across both figures, JIT consistently outperforms reactive correction at a single node. The size of the gap depends on whether the figure plots capacity or availability, and on which steering model is in use. A paragraph in the \emph{Discussion} now states exactly this, with explicit cross-references to all relevant figure subpanels.

\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{In Figure~3, after beam alignment, why is there a power deviation compared to the ``perfect alignment'' case? What does this deviation represent? Why does JIT's trajectory information not seem to assist in perfectly aligning the beam after realignment?}
}
\end{tcbremark}

\noindent The residual gap comes from the system's finite reaction latency: during $T_{\text{react}}$, the UE keeps drifting after the AP has issued its steering command. Eq.~(7) bounds this drift by $\eta \cdot \theta_{\text{HPBW}} = 0.36 \cdot \SI{13}{\degree} \approx \SI{4.7}{\degree}$ in the worst case (no prediction). In practice, JIT does not steer to the UE's last known position. Instead, the AP applies an EWMA filter to the IMU samples carried in the control frame, predicts where the UE will be when the steering command completes, and steers there. The actual residual is therefore much smaller than this worst-case bound, and the gain loss visible in Fig.~3 stays below \SI{1}{\deci\bel}, matching the small steady-state offset between JIT and Perfect Alignment. On hardware with non-zero $T_{\text{react}}$, the residual cannot be eliminated entirely. A new \emph{Post-alignment residual} paragraph in \emph{Results} states this quantitatively and ties the observation back to Eq.~(7).
