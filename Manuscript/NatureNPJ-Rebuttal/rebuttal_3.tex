\section*{Editor}
\markboth{Editor}{}

\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{Clearly differentiate your pre-failure trigger and coordinated AP/UE mechanism from prior predictive tracking and IMU-assisted beam management. Provide a more detailed related-work comparison beyond the two baselines.}
}
\end{tcbremark}

\noindent We have expanded the related-work positioning in the revised manuscript. The two reactive baselines (Hierarchical Search and IMU-Assist) remain the most directly comparable alternatives at the same protocol-level scope as JIT; JIT is now also contrasted against six families of prior work, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:reb_relatedwork}.\\

\noindent What is new in JIT relative to these families is the combination of UE-side IMU motion sensing, a pre-failure trigger that anticipates the next beam-edge crossing, and coordinated AP/UE realignment via a single in-band sub-THz control frame. To our knowledge, no prior work combines all three within a single sub-THz protocol with hardware-in-the-loop validation. 3GPP NR Beam Failure Recovery~\cite{Giordani2018ATO, Heng2021SixChallenges} sends a discrete PRACH-based recovery message only after the MAC entity has counted $N$ consecutive Beam Failure Instances at L1; RF-based proactive failure prediction~\cite{9838772, Alrabeiah2020DeepLearning} forecasts a failure from RF time-series or sub-6\,GHz features and triggers a standard beam switch, but uses no motion sensing and cannot anticipate rotation-induced edge crossings; AP-side multimodal sensing~\cite{Alkhateeb2023DeepSense, Jiang2023LiDAR, Morais2023Position} requires LiDAR, radar, vision, or GPS hardware at every AP; UE-side orientation-based work~\cite{Rezaie2022Orientation, Struye2023CoVRage} uses orientation primarily for codebook selection rather than to anticipate the next edge crossing; continuous IMU streaming~\cite{Brambilla_2019} exchanges IMU data on every tracking interval over a separate out-of-band control channel; and bilateral pilot-driven coordination~\cite{Alkhateeb2018DLCB, Xue2023Standardization, Heng2021SixChallenges} incurs overhead regardless of whether the link is at risk. JIT sends one short frame only when a crossing is imminent, for an overhead of less than $0.4\%$.\\

\noindent The corresponding manuscript change is the expanded related-work paragraph in the \emph{Introduction}, which now references the same six families with per-family limitations.

\noindent We also looked for prior mechanisms that, like JIT, transmit a single short pre-failure control frame to trigger coordinated AP/UE realignment. The closest standardized analogue is the 3GPP NR Beam Failure Recovery Request (BFRQ)~\cite{Giordani2018ATO, Heng2021SixChallenges}, sent on PRACH or a dedicated PUCCH MAC-CE; BFRQ is reactive and fires only after L1 has counted $N$ consecutive Beam Failure Instances. The closest research analogue is~\cite{9838772}, which trains a failure-prediction model on RF time-series features; the trigger is RF-symptom-based and contains no motion information. The closest sensor-side analogue is~\cite{Brambilla_2019}, which transmits IMU samples on every tracking interval over an out-of-band control link rather than a single in-band frame. The combination used by JIT---a single in-band sub-THz frame, motion-predictive, sent before any RF degradation---does not appear in these prior works.

\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{Analyze robustness of the trigger under long-term IMU drift without zero-velocity updates, and explain the effect of the 10\,ms IMU sampling period on sub-millisecond control reaction.}
}
\end{tcbremark}

\noindent The revised \emph{Methods/JIT Beam Realignment} subsection addresses both points. Figs.~\ref{fig:reb_imuperiod} and~\ref{fig:reb_zupt} support the analysis.\\

\noindent Two design choices bound the residual drift on both per-prediction and longer timescales. The trigger uses the Link Deviation \emph{velocity} (single accelerometer integration), not absolute position (double integration); over the AP's per-prediction window $T_{\text{react}} \approx \SI{15}{\micro\second}$, the residual position drift is $\sigma_p = \sigma_{a,n}\sqrt{T_{\text{react}}^{3}/3} \lesssim \SI{1}{\nano\meter}$ for the noise density in Table~2, an angular drift of order $10^{-8}$~degrees at $d_{\text{link}} = \SI{3}{m}$ and far below $\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$; this bound has no dependence on session length. The JIT control frame transmits only the displacement change since the last transmission, with the on-UE accumulator reset at each transmission, so cumulative drift inside the frame payload is bounded by a single inter-transmission interval rather than the full session duration.\\

\noindent Across multi-hour or multi-day operation, the initialization ZUPT can be invoked opportunistically: any interval during which the bias-corrected $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\| \approx 0$ persists for more than a few IMU samples (device on a desk, charging, or in a pocket between motion bursts) is an implicit zero-velocity interval and can be used to re-estimate $\mathbf{b}_a, \mathbf{b}_\omega$ to within the sensor noise floor without user action (Fig.~\ref{fig:reb_zupt}). For sustained continuous-mobility scenarios where such intervals are sparse, complementary bias-tracking from the inertial-navigation literature (learning-based dead-reckoning~\cite{Brossard2020AIIMU}, deep-learning drift compensation~\cite{Chen2024DLInertial}, automatic zero-velocity detection~\cite{Skog2010ZUPT}) would augment JIT's calibration as future work.\\

\noindent The IMU sampling period $T_{\text{IMU}}$ (\SI{10}{\milli\second} in Table~2; commodity IMUs such as the Bosch BMI270 sample at $\geq$\SI{1}{\kilo\hertz}) gates only the rate at which the trigger condition (Eq.~7) is evaluated. Once a trigger fires, the control-frame transmission ($T_{\text{frame}} \approx \SI{5}{\micro\second}$), the AP processing ($T_{\text{proc}} = \SI{10}{\micro\second}$), and the steering actuation proceed on their own clocks. Fig.~\ref{fig:reb_imuperiod} shows JIT's mean capacity is flat across $T_{\text{IMU}} \in [1, 15]$\,ms and degrades gradually thereafter; $T_{\text{IMU}}$ is not the control-reaction bottleneck across the commodity range.\\

\noindent If the SNR falls below \SI{9.59}{\deci\bel} the AP cannot decode the control frame and the system falls back to reactive recovery via Hierarchical Search, so the IMU prediction and the RF fallback are complementary rather than dependent.

\blue{Explain how the edge of coverage is defined and whether it adapts to UE speed or rotation. Also describe how the UE detects approaching EoC to transmit IMU data.}

\end{tcbremark}

\noindent A new \emph{Trigger Boundary and Edge of Coverage} subsubsection has been added at the head of \emph{Methods/JIT Beam Realignment}. Fig.~\ref{fig:reb_eoc} provides the geometric picture; a condensed version is also embedded in the manuscript.\\

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\input{Figures/RebFigB_EoC}
\caption{Schematic (not to scale) of the Edge of Coverage (EoC) and JIT's trigger boundary. The EoC lies at $\pm\theta_{\text{HPBW}}/2$ off boresight (\textcolor{cb_green!55!black}{green wedge edge}); the trigger boundary (\textcolor{cb_orange!85!black}{orange dashed edge}) sits inside the EoC, at the offset where the projected angular displacement over $T_{\text{react}}$ reaches $\eta\,\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$. The boundary is not a fixed-radius circle; it shifts toward boresight as $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\|$ grows.}
\label{fig:reb_eoc}
\end{figure}

\noindent The EoC is the angle off the AP's beam axis at which the antenna gain has dropped by $3$\,dB, i.e., $\pm\,\theta_{\text{HPBW}}/2$ off boresight. Crossing the EoC corresponds to a worst-case $6$\,dB round-trip gain loss, which is consequential in sub-THz where narrow, high-gain beams leave little SNR margin.\\

\noindent If JIT waited for the UE to reach the EoC, there would be no time left to signal the AP and complete coordinated steering within the beam-residence window. JIT therefore places a \emph{trigger boundary} inside the EoC, at the offset where the projected angular displacement over the system reaction time $T_{\text{react}}$ equals $\eta\,\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$. The boundary is not a circle of fixed radius; it is the set of UE positions where Eq.~(7) of the main paper holds with equality.\\

\noindent The boundary adapts to motion: $\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}$ unifies the UE's angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{UE}}$ and its LoS-perpendicular translational velocity $\mathbf{v}_{\perp}/d_{\text{link}}$ into a single 3D angular rate, so faster motion (larger $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\|$) makes the projected displacement reach $\eta\,\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$ at a smaller angular offset and fires the trigger earlier (boundary contracts toward boresight); slower motion expands the boundary toward the EoC and suppresses unnecessary control frames. Under pure rotation $\mathbf{v}_{\perp} \approx 0$ and the trigger fires off the rotational term alone; under pure translation $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{UE}} \approx 0$ and the trigger fires off the translational term alone.\\

\noindent For AP-stationary scenarios (e.g., a wall-mounted access point serving mobile UEs), the UE detects an approaching EoC locally, without AP-side feedback. At every IMU sample the UE applies the ZUPT-derived biases to obtain $\mathbf{a}_{\text{corr}}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{corr}}$, transforms its body-frame angular and translational velocities into the AP-anchored world frame via the orientation quaternion $\mathbf{q}_{\text{body}}$, computes $\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}$ using its current estimate of $d_{\text{link}}$ (from the last known-good RF reference), and tests whether $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\| \cdot T_{\text{react}} > \eta\,\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$. The required quantities are all available on the UE side (the IMU readings, the orientation quaternion, the last known $d_{\text{link}}$, and the antenna's $\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$), so no AP-side feedback is needed while the link is still inside the beam. If the trigger fires, the UE transmits a control frame; otherwise, it continues to monitor.\\

\noindent The trigger uses velocity rather than position, which avoids double-integration drift. Bias re-grounding via opportunistic ZUPT bounds the residual bias contribution. The RF-side SNR fallback (decoding threshold \SI{9.59}{\deci\bel}) means that if the IMU+RF prediction underestimates the crossing, JIT falls back to the Hierarchical Search baseline already characterized in Figs.~2--8 of the main paper rather than failing silently. For mobile-AP cases (e.g., UAV-borne access points), the same control frame is the natural carrier for the AP's kinematic state; this extension is listed in the validation-extension future work.

\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{The current validation scope remains simplified (single AP--single UE, predominantly LoS/FSPL-based modeling, and HIL constrained to the azimuth plane), which limits the external validity of some design insights, such as the robustness and low-overhead operation of JIT.}

\noindent We agree that this is first-order validation, and have made the scoping explicit in the revised \emph{Statistics and Reproducibility} subsection. The single-link, predominantly LoS configuration isolates the beam-management mechanism from the confounding effects of multi-AP, NLoS, or larger-scale settings.\\

\noindent The dual-axis rotary stages support full 3D antenna pointing in both azimuth and altitude; the HIL antenna pointing is therefore 3D. The constraint is on the linear translation of the RF front-end mounts: each front-end (PSG + AWG + upconverter on the Tx side, downconverter + oscilloscope on the Rx side) is too heavy to lift in the vertical direction, so the linear translation is restricted to the laboratory-floor azimuth plane. This is now stated explicitly in \emph{Statistics and Reproducibility}.\\

\noindent The robustness claim follows from the trigger condition (Eq.~7), not the HIL geometry: the condition is evaluated in 3D world coordinates, and the simulator exercises 3D motion in the rotational-acceleration, angular-displacement, and combined-motion sweeps (Figs.~4--8). The low-overhead claim ($O_{\text{sig}} < 0.4\%$) follows from the event-driven trigger and the IEEE 802.15.3d THz-SC frame budget, not the HIL constraint.\\

\noindent The future-work paragraph of \emph{Discussion} now lists five extensions: (i) multi-AP scheduling with handover, (ii) NLoS channel models with multipath and transient blockage, (iii) HIL trials in which both nodes translate through azimuth and altitude, (iv) sustained continuous-mobility scenarios with complementary bias-tracking mechanisms beyond the present initial-event focus, and (v) larger-scale measurement campaigns in indoor and outdoor environments.

\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{The Perlin smootherstep function in equation~(8) mathematically forces velocity and acceleration to zero at keyframe boundaries. While this is reasonable for generating smooth trajectories, the authors should explain why this is an adequate approximation for the considered mobility scenarios.}
}
\end{tcbremark}

\noindent The clarification has been added immediately after the smootherstep equation in \emph{Methods/MobileTHz Simulation Platform}. The zero-velocity and zero-acceleration boundary condition models pause-resume mobility patterns such as walking with intermediate stops or beginning and ending a turn from rest, which are the primary motion classes considered in this study. Pause-resume motion is also the hardest case for a predictive scheme: the velocity profile is unknown at the onset of motion, acceleration changes rapidly, and the system has the least time to react. Sustained-motion variants (e.g., walking at a steady speed) are easier to predict from a brief observation window, so the performance reported here lower-bounds what is achievable on those variants.\\

\noindent Both the software simulator and the HIL testbed support trajectories without a stationary boundary, such as continuous walking through multiple checkpoints, by chaining smootherstep-interpolated segments whose endpoints exchange non-zero velocities while preserving slot-by-slot $C^1$ continuity where required. The smootherstep choice is therefore a boundary condition selected for the hardest mobility events rather than a limitation on the framework.

\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{The setting of the ``trigger boundary'' in the JIT method is not well explained. Specifically, how is EoC determined, and will it be adjusted based on the user's speed or rotation?}

\end{tcbremark}

\noindent A new \emph{Trigger Boundary and Edge of Coverage} subsubsection has been added at the head of \emph{Methods/JIT Beam Realignment}; the geometric picture is in Fig.~\ref{fig:reb_eoc}. The full answer is in our response to \emph{Editor remark~4}. In short: the EoC is the angle off the AP's beam axis at which the antenna gain has dropped by $3$\,dB ($\pm\,\theta_{\text{HPBW}}/2$ off boresight); JIT triggers at an inner boundary defined by $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\| \cdot T_{\text{react}} = \eta\,\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$; the boundary adapts to motion (faster motion contracts it toward boresight, slower motion expands it toward the EoC). Pure rotation fires the trigger via the $\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{UE}}$ term; pure translation fires it via the $\mathbf{v}_{\perp}/d_{\text{link}}$ term.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------%
\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{Since the user is within the beam coverage, how can it determine that it is approaching the EoC in order to transmit its IMU message?}
}
\end{tcbremark}

\noindent Detection is performed locally at the UE, without AP-side RF feedback. At every IMU sample the UE applies the ZUPT-derived biases to obtain $\mathbf{a}_{\text{corr}}, \boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{corr}}$, transforms its body-frame velocities into the AP-anchored world frame via $\mathbf{q}_{\text{body}}$, computes $\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}$ using the last known-good $d_{\text{link}}$, and tests $\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\| \cdot T_{\text{react}} > \eta\,\theta_{\text{HPBW}}$. All four required quantities are available on the UE side (IMU readings, orientation quaternion, last known link distance, and the antenna's HPBW), so the UE can predict the future EoC crossing from its own kinematic state and transmit a control frame before the link is lost. The trigger uses velocity rather than position, which avoids double-integration drift; opportunistic ZUPT bounds the residual bias; and the RF-side SNR fallback at \SI{9.59}{\deci\bel} means that if the IMU+RF prediction underestimates the crossing, JIT falls back to the Hierarchical Search baseline rather than failing silently. For mobile-AP cases (e.g., UAV-borne access points), the same control frame is the natural carrier for the AP's kinematic state; this extension is in the future-work list.

\begin{tcbremark}
{\fontfamily{cmss}\selectfont
\blue{In Figure~2, the JIT method shows trends that seem to contradict the IMU-assisted approach at high acceleration. What limitations in the JIT method explain this divergence from IMU-assisted performance? In contrast, in Figure~4, why does JIT match the IMU trend in the electronic model? \noindent Fig.~2 and Fig.~4 plot different motion types (translational vs.\ rotational), and Fig.~5 adds a different metric (link availability rather than mean capacity). The apparent inconsistency comes from this difference, not from a methodological issue.\\

\noindent Fig.~2 sweeps translational acceleration with mean capacity. As acceleration grows, the beam residence time shrinks. IMU-Assist loses lock because its reactive search cannot converge within this shrinking window, while JIT's pre-failure trigger and AP/UE coordination preserve alignment, so the capacity gap widens with acceleration.\\

\noindent Fig.~4 sweeps rotational acceleration. In the Electronic model (Fig.~4(c)), the steering hardware is fast enough that IMU-Assist's local UE counter-rotation nearly keeps up with the motion, so the relative capacity gap narrows. JIT still keeps an absolute lead of about \SI{19}{Gbps} at \SI{700}{deg/s^2} because the AP holds its position rather than starting a misdirected scan. The link-availability view (Fig.~5) under the Ideal Mechanical model shows the same effect: only JIT sustains a usable link across the full rotational range.\\

\noindent JIT outperforms single-node correction across both figures; the size of the gap depends on the motion type, the chosen metric, and the steering model. The \emph{Discussion} now summarizes this: end-to-end AP/UE coordination, not single-side correction, drives the gains observed across all three regimes.
